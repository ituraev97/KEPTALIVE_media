## History of the idea
- R Adams Cowley: cardio-thoracic surgeon, University of Maryland; original unit had two beds, expanded to four, called the "death lab"; "there is a golden hour between life and death" (early interview); helicopter arrangement negotiated 1968; first medevac helicopter transport Mar. 19, 1970 (Maryland State Police). — EMS1, "How the 'death lab' evolved into a modern trauma system": https://www.ems1.com/trauma/articles/how-the-death-lab-evolved-into-a-modern-trauma-system-L5PhfJiEE93gKwPQ/
- Korea: Bell H-13 Sioux and Hiller H-23 Raven; H-13 pilots transported 18,000 of the war's 23,000 casualties to MASH units; first Army aerial medical evacuation missions Jan. 3, 1951 (1st Lt. Willis G. Shawn, 1st Lt. Joseph L. Bowler); death rate of evacuated patients before reaching medical facilities 4.5% (WWII) → 2.5% (Korea). — U.S. Army, "Army helicopters in Korea, 1950 to '53": https://www.army.mil/article/177302/army_helicopters_in_korea_1950_to_53
- Vietnam: 57th Medical Detachment (Helicopter Ambulance) arrived April 1962 with five UH-1 Hueys; in 1963 Maj. Lloyd E. Spencer chose "Dustoff" from the Navy Support Activity's call sign book; became permanent call sign for all helicopter ambulances. — Texas Tech Vietnam Center, Dustoff exhibit, "The Beginning": https://www.vietnam.ttu.edu/exhibits/dustoff/beginning.php
- Maj. Charles L. Kelly led the 57th in 1964; motto "No compromises. No rationalization. Fly the mission. Now."; died July 1, 1964 during an attempt to evacuate American advisors and South Vietnamese soldiers; "Not until I have all your wounded" (pilot Ernie Sylvester's oral history). — Texas Tech Vietnam Center, "The Dustoff Mystique": https://www.vietnam.ttu.edu/exhibits/dustoff/mystique.php
- Sept. 25, 1967 – July 31, 1968: six helicopters flew 8,644 missions and transported 21,431 patients (George Baum, former Flatiron commander). — U.S. Army: https://www.army.mil/article/197320
- "DUSTOFF" is the callsign specific to U.S. Army Air Ambulance units (2024). — DVIDS video 923203 description: https://www.dvidshub.net/video/923203

## The call
- 9-line request elements incl. landing-zone location, unit call sign, precedence, special equipment, number of patients by type; line number + phonetic letter/number "because it's quicker for brevity"; overclassification example (twisted ankle reported urgent wastes air assets a ground evacuation could handle); Hohenfels example: one heat casualty turned out to be four, aircraft reconfigured at the last minute; golden hour starts when flight crews receive the request and ends when the patient is dropped off at a medical treatment facility. — U.S. Army, "7ATC's Safety Focus: 9-Line Medevac": https://www.army.mil/article/189003/7atcs_safety_focus_9_line_medevac
- "The primary goal of medevac is to get the patient to a higher level of care inside of the 'Golden Hour'." — Rhode Island Army National Guard, DVIDS video 762387: https://www.dvidshub.net/video/762387

## The 60-minute rule (Kotwal et al., JAMA Surgery 2015)
- 2009: Secretary of Defense Robert M. Gates mandated ≤60 minutes (call to arrival at a treatment facility) for helicopter transport of critically injured U.S. casualties; previous goal 2 hours. 21,089 casualties, Afghanistan, Sept. 2001 – March 2014. KIA 16.0% (386/2,411) → 9.9% (964/9,755); CFR 13.7 → 7.6; DOW 4.1% → 4.3%; projected vs actual CFR = 359 lives saved (estimate); 4,542 casualties with detailed data: median transport 90 → 43 min; within 60 min 24.8% → 75.2%; KIA lower among critically injured who received blood transfusion and were transported within 60 min; CFR decline "associated with" increasing % transported within 60 min. — ScienceDaily (JAMA Network release): https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2015/09/150930140123.htm
- Pentagon introduced the policy in 2009; previous standard two hours; lead author Dr. Russ Kotwal, U.S. Army Institute of Surgical Research; published online Sept. 30, 2015. — HealthDay: https://www.healthday.com/health-news/general-health/u-s-combat-deaths-declined-under-golden-hour-policy-study-703685.html

## Civilian evidence
- Newgard CD et al., "Emergency medical services intervals and survival in trauma: assessment of the 'golden hour' in a North American prospective cohort," Ann Emerg Med 2010;55:235–246: 3,665 patients, 146 EMS agencies, 51 Level I/II trauma centers; no significant association between any EMS interval and mortality. Commentary: most total EMS times under ~47 minutes; longest-time quartile had far more helicopter transports (possible confounding). — JEMS: https://www.jems.com/ems-training/ems-intervals-associated-morta/

## When the hour runs out
- DARPA: golden hour may become unattainable (degraded communications, contested airspace, advanced weaponry); the Live Chain = self-aid, buddy-aid, medic care, en route care, surgical intervention. — DVIDS 993660 description: https://www.dvidshub.net/video/993660
- Fort Riley: Irwin Army Community Hospital and the 1st Infantry Division prepare to provide patient care for extended periods when evacuation is not available. — DVIDS 810194 ("Holding a Life Longer"): https://www.dvidshub.net/video/810194
