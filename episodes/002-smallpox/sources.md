# Sources — KEPT ALIVE · OUTBREAK 002 · Smallpox: How the World Erased a Disease
All pages opened during research on 2026-11-03 (UTC). Key facts extracted per source.

1. CDC, "History of Smallpox" — https://www.cdc.gov/smallpox/history/history.html
   - Existed at least 3,000 years; smallpox-like rashes on Egyptian mummies. Earliest written description China, 4th century CE; India 7th c.; Asia Minor 10th c.
   - On average about 3 in 10 people who got it died.
   - Spread: Japan 6th c.; northern Africa, Spain, Portugal 7th c.; Europe 11th c.; Caribbean & Americas 16th–17th c.; Australia 18th c.
   - Variolation: material from smallpox sores, scratched into the arm or inhaled; fewer died than from natural infection.
   - 1796 Jenner inoculated an 8-year-old boy with cowpox; boy did not develop smallpox after later exposure. 1801 treatise quote: "the annihilation of the smallpox, the most dreadful scourge of the human species, must be the final result of this practice."
   - During the 1800s the vaccine virus changed from cowpox to vaccinia.
   - 1959 WHO global eradication plan; struggled (funding, staff, commitment, vaccine); still widespread in 1966. Intensified Eradication Program 1967 (freeze-dried vaccine, bifurcated needle, case surveillance, mass vaccination).
   - Eliminated: North America 1952, Europe 1953, South America 1971, Asia 1975, Africa 1977.
   - Late 1975: a 3-year-old girl in Bangladesh, last person known to have naturally acquired variola major. Oct 30, 1977: hospital cook in Merca, Somalia, diagnosed with variola minor — last naturally acquired; recovered. (Names not used on air — channel rule.)
   - 1978: medical photographer at Birmingham University Medical School infected, died Sept 11, 1978; infection linked to the building where smallpox research was conducted.
   - May 8, 1980: 33rd World Health Assembly declared the world free of smallpox. "Many people consider smallpox eradication to be the biggest achievement in international public health."
   - 1981 four countries held variola (US, England, Russia, South Africa); by 1984 England and South Africa destroyed or transferred stocks; today only two WHO-supervised locations: CDC Atlanta and VECTOR, Koltsovo, Russia.
2. WHO, "Smallpox" (emergencies/situations) — https://www.who.int/emergencies/situations/smallpox
   - Caused by variola virus; existed at least 3000 years; Jenner's 1796 vaccine "the first successful vaccine to be developed"; WHO intensified plan 1967; last natural case Somalia 1977; 1980 declared eradicated — "the only infectious disease to achieve this distinction"; Smallpox Secretariat: biennial biosafety/biosecurity inspections of the 2 variola repositories; smallpox vaccine emergency stockpile; annual reports to WHA.
3. U.S. National Library of Medicine, "Smallpox: A Great and Terrible Scourge" — variolation: https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/smallpox/sp_variolation.html
   - Developed in Asia; dried scabs blown into the nose; 1–2% of variolated died vs 30% natural; spread to Africa, India, Ottoman Empire by 1700; Europeans/Americans used skin puncture; Lady Mary Wortley Montagu learned of it 1717 in Constantinople (husband British ambassador); 1721 prisoners and abandoned children inoculated at her urging and the Princess of Wales's — none got the disease on later exposure; first tried in Boston 1721 epidemic; Cotton Mather learned of it from his slave Onesimus.
4. NLM, vaccination page — https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/smallpox/sp_vaccination.html
   - Jenner recognized dairymaids infected with cowpox were immune; 1796 deliberately infected 8-year-old boy with cowpox, then exposed him to smallpox — did not get it; repeated on other children incl. his own son; findings published 1798; translated into Portuguese, French, Dutch, Italian, Japanese within 20 years; 1807 Bavaria first to require vaccination of recruits.
5. NLM, eradication page — https://nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/smallpox/sp_eradicate.html
   - 1961 bifurcated needle: one-quarter of the vaccine previously needed, "takes" nearly 100%, reusable up to 100 times after sterilization; 1967 WHO workers vaccinated 25 million people but large segments missed; Foege's Eradication Escalation (E2): all-out push in October, seasonal low; 1968 trial in Sierra Leone, then most smallpox-infested country; zero-pox within nine months.
6. Breman JG, "Donald Ainslie (D. A.) Henderson … Smallpox Eradication: Leadership and Legacy," J Infect Dis 2017;215(5):673–676 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5388283
   - Henderson led WHO Intensified Smallpox Eradication Programme from October 1966 (Geneva), trained under Alexander Langmuir at CDC; 1967: endemic in 33 countries, population 1.2 billion; 10–15 million cases a year, <5% reported; only heat-stable freeze-dried vaccine used; bifurcated needle invented by Benjamin Rubin of Wyeth Laboratories; Foege showed ring vaccination/surveillance-containment "vastly better than indiscriminate mass vaccination"; last naturally transmitted case Oct 1977; >150,000 workers; >700 "lieutenants" from 73 countries (>150 from CDC); Order of the Bifurcated Needle (~1976), >700 needles twisted into lapel pins.
7. Military Health System (health.mil), "Gen. George Washington Ordered Smallpox Inoculations for All Troops" — https://health.mil/News/Articles/2021/08/16/Gen-George-Washington-Ordered-Smallpox-Inoculations-for-All-Troops
   - Est. 90% of Continental Army deaths caused by disease; less than a quarter of colonial troops had had the virus, most English troops immune; Feb 6, 1777 letter to Dr. William Shippen: "I have determined that the troops shall be inoculated."; feared backfire and harm to recruitment; "largely successful," according to the Library of Congress. Method: incision rubbed with material carrying smallpox.
8. American Battlefield Trust, "Washington Inoculates an Army" — https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/washington-inoculates-army
   - Smallpox ravaged the American army that invaded Canada in winter 1775–76; Feb 6, 1777 order; winter chosen because fighting had ebbed; most inoculations at Morristown, N.J., and Philadelphia; less than a quarter had contracted it naturally.
9. DoD News Release No. 634-02, Dec. 13, 2002, "DoD Details Military Smallpox Vaccination Program" (mirror) — https://www.globalsecurity.org/wmd/library/news/usa/2002/wmd-021213-dod01.htm
   - Routine civilian vaccination in U.S. ended around 1972; military routine vaccination limited to recruits in 1984, discontinued 1990; reassessment after 9/11 and anthrax letters; designated personnel vaccinated, starting with smallpox response teams and hospital/clinic workers.
10. CIDRAP, "FDA approves Acambis smallpox vaccine" (Sept 4, 2007) — https://www.cidrap.umn.edu/smallpox/fda-approves-acambis-smallpox-vaccine
   - FDA approval announced Sept 1 (2007); ACAM2000 uses vaccinia virus; grown in cell culture; derived from Dryvax, grown on the skin of calves; Acambis had supplied 192.5 million doses to CDC for the Strategic National Stockpile; FDA: intended for people at high risk of exposure.
11. U.S. Army Medical Research and Development Command, "Info Paper: USAMRIID" — https://mrdc.health.mil/assets/docs/media/publications/Info-Paper-USAMRIID.pdf
   - Established 1969 at Fort Detrick, Md.; only DoD laboratory equipped to safely study highly hazardous viruses at BSL-4; ran the pivotal Phase 3 trial leading to FDA approval of a new smallpox vaccine; key studies for the first FDA-approved small-molecule smallpox treatment.
