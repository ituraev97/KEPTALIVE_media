## Blood basics
- MedlinePlus (NIH/NLM), Blood — https://medlineplus.gov/blood.html
  - Plasma "is made of water, salts, and protein"; plasma makes up more than half of blood.
  - "Red blood cells (RBC) deliver oxygen from your lungs to your tissues and organs."
  - "White blood cells (WBC) fight infection and are part of your immune system."
  - "Platelets help blood to clot when you have a cut or wound."
  - "There are four blood types: A, B, AB, or O." Blood type "is important if you need a blood transfusion."
- NHLBI, Types of Blood Transfusions (archived 2008) — https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080917140215/http:/www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/dci/Diseases/bt/bt_types.html
  - "Blood is transfused either as whole blood (with all its parts) or, more often, as individual parts."
- Nobel Prize, Karl Landsteiner facts — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1930/landsteiner/facts
  - 1930 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for his discovery of human blood groups".
  - Reported in 1901 that people have different types of red blood cells; mixing blood sometimes made red cells stick together, which explained why early transfusions often went badly.
- Nobel Prize, Speed read: Blood relations — https://nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1930/speedread
  - Early transfusions "highly unpredictable", often a "hazardous and potentially fatal reaction".
  - Reactions occurred when the recipient had natural antibodies against the donor's cells. Groups A, B and O; AB found soon after.

## Low titer O, walking blood banks
- Eve Meinhardt, "'Walking blood banks' fill gap for medical care in field environment," army.mil, Oct. 8, 2019 — https://www.army.mil/article/224502/walking_blood_banks_fill_gap_for_medical_care_in_field_environment
  - Low titer O = type O soldiers with low antibody levels; standard "1 to 256". Type O donors "are already considered universal donors for packed red blood cells."
  - "Low titer O blood can be given to anyone in need, regardless of their blood type."
  - "Each of the identified Soldiers is regularly tested for a variety of blood-borne diseases."
  - Lt. Col. Melanie Sloan (director, Fort Bragg Blood Donor Center): "Everyone has antibodies. They are naturally occurring and can attach themselves to transfused blood cells."
  - 1st Lt. Robert Blough (PA, 82nd Airborne DIVARTY, former Special Forces medical sergeant): in 2007 treated an Iraqi man shot in the lower abdomen, "bleeding out internally"; "I did not have the ability to transfuse blood to save his life."
  - Lt. Col. George Barbee (deputy corps surgeon, XVIII Airborne Corps): Army moved from whole blood to component therapy in the 1970s; components not "a feasible option for an immediate need for blood in the field." Whole blood used in 1918 (WWI), WWII, Korea, Vietnam.
- Maj. Tony Mayne, "Ranger whole blood program wins an Army's Greatest Innovation Award," army.mil, March 14, 2017 — https://www.army.mil/article/184219/ranger_whole_blood_program_wins_an_armys_greatest_innovation_award
  - 75th Ranger Regiment ROLO (Ranger O Low Titer) program; group O low titer = IgM titers less than 1:256; low anti-A/anti-B titers carry low or negligible risk of a catastrophic acute hemolytic reaction in recipients not of the same group.
  - "went from concept to implementation at the unit-level in only 18 months"; follows WWII, Korea, Vietnam blood programs.
- Jessica Pellegrini (ASBP), "ASBP's low titer Type O whole blood helps save lives on the battlefield," Keesler AFB (undated) — https://www.keesler.af.mil/News/Article-Display/Article/1206400/
  - Group O donors prescreened for IgM antibodies to group A and group B blood; low titer if below 1:256; also screened for infectious disease; then "considered universal and safe to be transfused to anyone regardless of the injured patient's blood type."
  - Not separated into three components.
  - Lt. Col. Ethan Miles (75th Ranger Regiment surgeon): hemorrhagic shock, "the leading cause of potentially survivable death on the battlefield"; cleared donors are "a walking blood bank on target."
  - May 2015 Army Blood Program prescreened the first Ranger Regiment donors; several months later Rangers deployed using prescreened donors; March 2016 ASBBC-Pacific Northwest (JBLM) sent first shipment to Afghanistan; concept to implementation 18 months; more than 1,000 units provided to CENTCOM (Col. Audra Taylor).
  - Taylor: whole blood "can be stored in a standard refrigerator and used at all roles of care"; avoids logistics of frozen plasma and platelets.
- Sgt. Sarah Stegall, "Valkyrie: emergency fresh whole blood transfusion enhances 15th MEU medical capabilities," marines.mil, Feb. 2, 2021 — https://www.marines.mil/News/News-Display/Article/2489777/valkyrie-emergency-fresh-whole-blood-transfusion-enhances-15th-meu-medical-capa/
  - Navy adopted emergency fresh whole blood transfusion from the Army Ranger program; developed by Lt. Cmdr. Russell Wier in 2017.
  - Volunteers tested pre-mission for blood type, low titer status and transmissible transfusion-related diseases, added to a donor roster.
  - In training, blood drawn into a donor bag "goes back into the volunteer".
  - Lt. Cmdr. Michael Hight: fresh whole blood needs no refrigeration; "already in our bodies at 98.6 degrees."
- Cpl. Juan Torres, "U.S. Navy, ADF participate in Valkyrie emergency fresh whole blood transfusion training," DVIDS, May 26, 2024 — https://dvidshub.net/news/472274/us-navy-adf-participate-valkyrie-emergency-fresh-whole-blood-transfusion-training
  - "Valkyrie is our emergency fresh whole blood program"; developed by Lt. Cmdr. Russell Wier in 2017, inspired by the Army Ranger O Low Titer program; teaches "how to do live blood transfusions in the field".
- LCpl Talan Werner, "2nd Marine Logistics Group hosts walking blood bank training," DVIDS, March 18, 2026 — https://www.dvidshub.net/news/560832/2nd-marine-logistics-group-hosts-walking-blood-bank-training-enhanced-battlefield-care
  - Walking blood bank uses "fellow service members as donors"; course covers "managing donor rosters to performing transfusions"; JTS-certified course implements a new DoD-wide standard for fresh whole blood transfusions; Feb. 23–27, 2026.
- Curtis Hill, "Navy medical personnel aboard MCB Camp Pendleton train on walking blood bank operations at NHCP," DVIDS, May 12, 2026 — https://www.dvidshub.net/news/565066/navy-medical-personnel-aboard-mcb-camp-pendleton-train-walking-blood-bank-operations-nhcp
  - Lt. Gildas Noumen (organizer): trained personnel must be capable of screening donors and completing blood draws "in under 28 minutes" (two-day training event).
  - Lt. Cmdr. Jeff Hebert (laboratory department head, NMC San Diego): "One patient can wipe out an entire blood supply."

## History
- Proto Magazine (Mass General), "The Blood Depot," June 13, 2017 — https://protomag-dev.partners.org/?p=13318
  - Oswald Hope Robertson, Army Medical Corps; donors "usually lightly wounded soldiers" with type O blood; about two cups each; sodium citrate; ammunition boxes with sawdust and ice, kept "for a few weeks"; tested at the Battle of Cambrai; "Not all soldiers who received the transfusions from the blood depot survived."; by March 1918 AEF hospitals "officially adopted Robertson's method".
- Elizabeth Yuko, "How World War I Spurred the Invention of Blood Banks," History.com, Nov. 5, 2024 — https://history.com/news/how-world-war-i-spurred-the-invention-of-blood-banks
  - Robertson, U.S. Army doctor, set up the first blood depot, an ice chest stocked with flasks of blood; "The first successful transfusion from this early blood bank model took place in 1917."
- Review, Trauma Surgery & Acute Care Open, vol. 11, e002278 — https://tsaco.bmj.com/content/11/2/e002278
  - More than 10,000 WB transfusions estimated in 1918 alone.
  - WWII: military and American Red Cross collected "more than 13 million pints of WB"; March 1945: up to 62,000 units shipped to European and Pacific theaters.
  - Korea: no operational military blood program at the start; "the first blood shipments did not arrive until more than 2 months into combat operations."
  - Vietnam: "between 1965 and 1971 approximately 1.3 million units of WB were shipped to Vietnam" "with roughly 600 000 units transfused to more than 330 000 US and allied casualties"; whole blood and walking blood banks central.
  - Shift away from whole blood "not driven by clinical evidence, but by economic, logistical, and perceived safety advantages of blood fractionation"; no prospective studies compared at the time.
  - "The military re-adopted warm FWB through WBBs in 2002 and, in 2017, reintroduced LTOWB to simplify resuscitation".
  - LTOWB "can be safely transfused to patients of any ABO type"; whole blood best seen as initial therapy for life-threatening hemorrhage, with transition to type-specific components once bleeding is controlled or LTOWB supply runs low.
- Armed Services Blood Program, Talking Points (archived 2016) — https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20161019064551/http:/www.militaryblood.dod.mil/press/Documents/Print_Talking_Points.pdf
  - "Stood up in 1952 by President Truman"; tri-service; after the Korean War took over military blood collection, processing and transport from the American Red Cross.

## Civilian
- Mayo Clinic, "A reemerging role for whole blood in modern trauma care," May 7, 2022 — https://mayoclinic.org/medical-professionals/trauma/news/a-reemerging-role-for-whole-blood-in-modern-trauma-care/mac-20531706
  - Civilian medicine began using components in the 1970s and 1980s; Stubbs: "Division into components allowed donations to benefit three patients rather than one."
  - Whole blood provides a 1-1-1 ratio; Stubbs: "Whole blood is the perfect balanced resuscitation blood product."
  - Stubbs and Jenkins brought whole blood to Southern Minnesota in 2015 for air ambulances; pediatric patients as young as 4 months from September 2021.
  - Whole blood has a shorter shelf life than components.
  - About 15% to 20% of trauma patients receive transfusions; about 3% massive transfusions.
  - "Blood cannot currently be manufactured", transfusions depend on human donors.
