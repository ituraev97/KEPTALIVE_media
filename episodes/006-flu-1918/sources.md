# Sources — KEPT ALIVE · OUTBREAK 006 — The Flu That Went to War (1918)
Every fact below was read on the page cited (opened 2026-11-07). Quotes are as returned from the page.

## CDC
- CDC, 1918 Pandemic (H1N1 virus) (archived) — https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/flu/pandemic-resources/1918-pandemic-h1n1.html
  - "The 1918 influenza pandemic was the most severe pandemic in recent history." Caused by an H1N1 virus with genes of avian origin.
  - "Although there is not universal consensus regarding where the virus originated, it spread worldwide during 1918-1919."
  - "In the United States, it was first identified in military personnel in spring 1918."
  - "It is estimated that about 500 million people or one-third of the world's population became infected with this virus."
  - "The number of deaths was estimated to be at least 50 million worldwide with about 675,000 occurring in the United States."
  - "Mortality was high in people younger than 5 years old, 20-40 years old, and 65 years and older."
  - "The high mortality in healthy people, including those in the 20-40 year age group, was a unique feature of this pandemic."
  - "With no vaccine to protect against influenza infection and no antibiotics to treat secondary bacterial infections", control efforts were limited to "isolation, quarantine, good personal hygiene, use of disinfectants, and limitations of public gatherings, which were applied unevenly."
  - Site navigation links CDC's page titled "The Deadliest Flu: The Complete Story of the Discovery and Reconstruction of the 1918 Pandemic Virus" (title used for "deadliest flu").
- CDC, History of 1918 Flu Pandemic (archived) — https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/flu/pandemic-resources/1918-commemoration/1918-pandemic-history.htm (same core statements as above)
- CDC, 1918 Pandemic Influenza: Three Waves (archived) — https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/flu/pandemic-resources/1918-commemoration/three-waves.htm
  - First outbreak of flu-like illness detected in the U.S. in March 1918, more than 100 cases at Camp Funston, Kansas.
  - "The mass troop movement contributed to the global spread of flu."
  - "This highly fatal second wave was responsible for most of the U.S. deaths attributed to the pandemic." Peak in fall 1918.
  - With many health professionals in the military, "shortages of medical personnel around the U.S."
  - Third wave winter and spring 1919; three waves "starting in March 1918 and subsiding by summer of 1919."
- CDC FluView, Weekly U.S. Influenza Surveillance Report, Week 42 ending Oct. 24, 2026 — https://www.cdc.gov/fluview/surveillance/2026-week-42.html
  - Activity "remains low nationally but is increasing in many parts of the country", ahead of the usual seasonal pace.
  - FluSurv-NET weekly hospitalization rate: CDC calls it the highest Week 42 rate since the 2010-11 season.
  - "Annual seasonal influenza (flu) vaccination is recommended"; "Influenza vaccines are available for persons aged 6 months and older" (as quoted by the independent fact-check).

## Military history
- Eric Durr, "Flu outbreak killed 45,000 U.S. Soldiers during World War I," National Guard Bureau, Aug. 30, 2018 — https://www.nationalguard.mil/News/Article-View/Article/1616713/flu-outbreak-killed-45000-us-soldiers-during-world-war-i
  - "About 45,000 American Soldiers died of influenza and related pneumonia by the end of 1918." "American combat deaths in World War I totaled 53,402."
  - Haskell County, Kansas: medical historians say the virus likely began there; local physician Loring Miner observed it in Jan./Feb. 1918 and alerted the U.S. Public Health Service in March 1918; young men from Haskell County trained at Camp Funston.
  - Camp Funston: first influenza cases identified March 4, 1918; "Within three weeks, 1,100 of the 56,222 troops at the camp were sick."
  - March 18: Camps Forrest and Greenleaf in Georgia reported cases; by end of April, 24 of the Army's 36 main camps reported outbreaks; April 1918 influenza hit Brest, France, a major port for American troops.
  - Camp Devens: influenza appeared September 1918; "By the end of the month, 14,000 Soldiers at the camp were sick", 757 died.
  - Camp Upton: appeared Sept. 13; Col. John S. Mallory shut the camp to civilian visitors and ended soldiers' leaves; epidemic ended Oct. 22, by then 6,131 hospitalized and 404 died.
  - Camp Syracuse: no hospital and only three ambulances; 2,289 flu cases among an average strength of 12,000; 208 soldiers died; 900 Syracuse residents died by the end of the epidemic.
  - The Army stopped taking in new draftees in October until the epidemic ended.
  - Army Surgeon General recommended halting troop shipments to France; President Wilson declined; Gen. Pershing demanded reinforcements and Wilson agreed.
  - Troopships sailed with healthy soldiers who often fell ill during the 10 to 14 day journey; a sailor aboard the S.S. Wilhelmina counted 15 burials at sea from the S.S. Grant on one day.
  - AEF in Europe in 1918 hospitalized 340,000 soldiers for influenza; 227,000 hospitalized from battle wounds.
  - October 1918, Meuse-Argonne offensive: 1,451 Americans died from flu. The 3rd Infantry Division evacuated more soldiers from the front with influenza than with combat wounds.
- "How Soldiers at Fort Riley Helped Spread the Spanish Flu Pandemic During WWI," Military.com, Feb. 16, 2026 — https://365.military.com/daily-news/investigations-and-features/2026/02/16/how-soldiers-fort-riley-helped-spread-spanish-flu-pandemic-during-wwi.html
  - "At its peak, Camp Funston processed nearly 56,000 troops preparing for deployment to France."
  - "Nations fighting in World War I suppressed press coverage of the outbreak to protect morale." Neutral Spain reported openly, including that King Alfonso XIII had fallen sick.
  - "In March alone, 84,000 American troops sailed for France." "The 15th U.S. Cavalry suffered 36 influenza cases and six deaths on a single Atlantic crossing."
  - "AEF statistics showed that 340,000 troops were hospitalized for influenza during 1918." "227,000 were admitted for combat wounds."
- McKinsey, McKinsey & Enriquez, "The 1918 Influenza in Missouri: Centennial Remembrance of the Crisis," Missouri Medicine 115(3), 2018 — https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6140156
  - "the mobilization of more than four million American military recruits"; 30 hastily built cantonments, each housing up to 50,000 soldiers in crowded barracks.
  - "757 previously robust young soldiers at Camp Devins died."
  - Camp Funston: in the following weeks "thousands of soldiers developed influenza and 38 died."
  - Roughly half of Missouri's physicians were serving in the military during the epidemic.
  - Approximately 670,000 Americans died; this exceeded the cumulative combat deaths of the U.S. military in both World Wars, the Korean conflict and the Vietnam War.
  - Highest mortality rates among persons 20 to 40 years old, an age group usually less affected by seasonal flu.
- Arlington National Cemetery, "Commemorating the Nurses of the 1918 Influenza Pandemic" — https://www.arlingtoncemetery.mil/Blog/Post/10832/Commemorating-the-Nurses-of-the-1918-Influenza-Pandemic
  - "The Army Nurse Corps reached an estimated peak strength of over 21,000" by the Armistice, Nov. 11, 1918.
  - Historians "generally estimate it to be somewhere above 200" American nurses who died during the war; most deaths attributed to influenza or complications such as pneumonia.
- Becky Little, "Why the 1918 Flu Was Called 'Spanish Flu'," History.com — https://www.history.com/articles/1918-pandemic-spanish-flu-censorship
  - The name is "a misnomer"; "Spain remained neutral throughout World War I and its press freely reported its flu cases", including "the Spanish king Alfonso XIII contracted it in the spring of 1918"; this "led to the misperception that the flu had originated or was at its worst in Spain."
  - Lora Vogt (National WWI Museum and Memorial): "Basically, it gets called the 'Spanish flu' because the Spanish media did their job."

## What killed them (NIH)
- NIH News, "Bacterial Pneumonia Caused Most Deaths in 1918 Influenza Pandemic," Aug. 19, 2008 (archived) — https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20080920083725/http://www.nih.gov/news/health/aug2008/niaid-19.htm
  - "most victims succumbed to bacterial pneumonia following influenza virus infection"; the virus often caused lung damage that let bacteria cause fatal pneumonia.
  - Researchers "examined lung tissue samples from 58 soldiers who died of influenza at various U. S. military bases in 1918 and 1919", preserved in paraffin blocks; a literature review covered 8,398 autopsies from 15 countries.
  - Morens et al., "Predominant role of bacterial pneumonia as a cause of death in pandemic influenza," J Infect Dis, Oct. 1, 2008.
  - Preparedness should include provisions to stockpile antibiotics and bacterial vaccines, in addition to influenza vaccines and antivirals.

## Home front
- "Rapid Response Was Crucial to Containing the 1918 Flu Pandemic," Infection Control Today, Apr. 3, 2007 (reporting two NIH-funded studies in PNAS) — https://www.infectioncontroltoday.com/view/rapid-response-was-crucial-containing-1918-flu-pandemic
  - St. Louis introduced a broad set of measures "within two days of the first reported cases"; Philadelphia imposed bans "more than two weeks after the first infections were reported" and "allowed a city-wide parade to take place prior to imposing their bans."
  - Peak mortality in St. Louis was one-eighth of Philadelphia's.
  - Cities that imposed multiple measures within a few days of first cases cut peak weekly death rates by up to half compared with cities that waited weeks. Studies covered 17 and 16 U.S. cities.
- RFE/RL, Philadelphia vs. St. Louis — https://thehub.rferl.org/a/lockdown-lessons-philadelphia-versus-st-louis-amid-1918-spanish-flu/30540105.html (parade Sept. 28, 1918; background only)

## The vaccine
- Military Infectious Diseases Research Program (MIDRP, health.mil), Influenza — https://midrp.health.mil/info/influenza.jsp
  - "The Army established the Board for the Investigation and Control of Influenza and other epidemic diseases in 1941."
  - Vaccine strains from "Commission on Influenza, U.S. Army, School of Public Health, University of Michigan" (Dr. Thomas Francis, Jr.); "This inactivated vaccine was studied in military recruits and college students." "Date of Licensure: November 1945" (Parke, Davis and Company).
  - "Between 1961 and 1993, the AFEB made 53 separate recommendations regarding the composition of influenza vaccines."
- University of Michigan Heritage Project, "The First Flu Shot" — https://heritage.umich.edu/stories/the-first-flu-shot
  - "the Army tapped him [Francis] to direct its Commission on Influenza"; Salk worked under Francis, day-to-day lead on the influenza work.
  - "They arranged to test 12,500 subjects, all of them participants in the Army Specialized Training Program", 1943-44 flu season, double-blind.
  - Orders "to inoculate every member of the U.S. Army in the fall of 1945".
