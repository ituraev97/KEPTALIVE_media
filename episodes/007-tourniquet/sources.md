# Sources — 007 HOW IT WORKS · The Tourniquet (all opened 2026-11-08)

1. Kragh JF Jr, Swan KG, Smith DC, Mabry RL, Blackbourne LH. "Historical review of emergency tourniquet use to stop bleeding." American Journal of Surgery 2012;203(2):242–252. https://www.east.org/content/documents/MilitaryResources/TCCC/TCCC%20Kragh%20Historical%20Rev%20of%20Emerg%20TQs%20AM%20J%20Surg%202012.pdf
   - Petit (1718) screw device, coined "tourniquet" from French tourner (to turn). Morel 1674 tourniquet at the siege of Besançon. Esmarch 1873: bandage wrapped around the limb that exsanguinated veins, occluded arteries and controlled bleeding (he called it a bandage).
   - Civil War: Confederate surgeon J. Julian Chisholm (1861): "troops too often used tourniquets needlessly."
   - WWI (1916): Major Blackwood, RAMC: "tourniquets are an invention of the Evil One"; "many limbs have been lost during this campaign by the indiscriminate use of them."
   - WWII: Watson-Jones: "More limbs have been lost by the use of tourniquets than have been saved." (Jolly 1941, Spanish Civil War, similar.)
   - Vietnam: Maughon (1970): "approximately 7.4% or 2,590 lives could be saved with better prehospital care."
   - 2003: the Army Surgeon General recommended the Combat Application Tourniquet (CAT) as standard issue.
   - Kragh study: survival 89% prehospital vs 78% hospital use; 87% with tourniquet vs 0% without.
2. Maher D. "Innovations from the Battlefield: Tourniquets." Journal of Military and Veterans' Health 2020;28(4). https://jmvh.org/?p=5942
   - Romans used narrow bronze straps; Galen criticised tourniquets as forcing more blood from a wound. Petit, tourner.
   - 1862 US Civil War: Samuel Gross made the first recorded recommendation to issue a tourniquet-like device to soldiers; criticism followed, often linked to training gaps and transport delays.
   - WWI British manual: "The systematic use of the elastic tourniquet cannot be too severely condemned."
   - WWII: Wolff and Adkins reviewed over 200 wounded service members with tourniquets; no gangrene, thromboembolic events or nerve damage directly attributable to the devices.
   - Vietnam: exsanguination from compressible extremity injuries was the leading cause of potentially preventable battlefield deaths; "estimated that over 2600 lives, or 7.4% of casualties, may have been saved with better pre-hospital care."
   - 1970s–early 1980s: US Army medics "were instructed that in almost all instances, amputation would follow tourniquet use." Emergency War Surgery: "Tourniquets are rarely needed for the control of haemorrhage and should be used only when all other methods fail."
   - 1991 Desert Storm: of 143 soldiers treated in US Army hospitals, three died (2.1%), each death involved limb haemorrhage.
   - Frank Butler: former Navy SEAL platoon commander and ophthalmologist.
   - 2003 Army Surgeon General Kevin Kiley recommended the CAT as standard issue for all deploying US service members.
   - (Maher's '13% of SOF KIA' misreads Holcomb 2007 — NOT used; see source 13.)
   - Boston 2013 (Boston Trauma Collaborative): 27 tourniquets applied among 66 patients with extremity injuries. (Survival claim not used: conflicting framing with King et al.)
3. Butler FK. "The U.S. Military Experience with Tourniquets and Hemostatic Dressings in the Afghanistan and Iraq Conflicts." Journal of Special Operations Medicine 2015;15(4). https://jsomonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/20154149Butler.pdf
   - TCCC work began 1993; paper published in Military Medicine in 1996.
   - "In 2005 and 2006, tourniquet use expanded rapidly throughout the U.S. military."
   - Lives saved by tourniquets estimated "between 1,000 and 2,000," based on the work of Col. John Kragh and colleagues.
   - Preventable extremity-hemorrhage deaths "dropped from the 7.8 percent noted in the previously mentioned Kelly study to 2.6 percent, a decrease of 67 percent" (Eastridge 2012 vs Kelly 2008).
   - TCCC Transition Initiative "documented 67 uses of tourniquets" in special operations units, with no limb losses to tourniquet ischemia.
4. Kragh JF Jr, Walters TJ, Baer DG, Fox CJ, Wade CE, Salinas J, Holcomb JB. "Survival with emergency tourniquet use to stop bleeding in major limb trauma." Annals of Surgery 2009;249(1):1–7 (abstract, NAEMT TCCC reference copy). https://www.naemt.org/docs/default-source/education-documents/tccc/tccc-updates_092017/tccc-reference-materials/07-tccc-key-publications-(abstracts)/kragh-survival-with-emerg-tourniquets-ann-surg-2009.pdf?sfvrsn=2
   - Prospective survey, combat support hospital, Baghdad, 7 months in 2006. 2,838 casualties with major limb trauma; 232 (8%) had tourniquets, 428 applied on 309 limbs; 31 (13%) died.
   - Prehospital: 194 patients, 22 died (11%); ED: 38 patients, 9 died (24%).
   - Five casualties with indications but no tourniquet: 0% survival vs 87% for those who received one.
   - Four (1.7%) transient nerve palsies; no amputations resulted solely from tourniquet use.
5. Crisp J. (Spc.), MNC-I PAO. "New tourniquet being issued to Soldiers in Iraq, Afghanistan." DVIDS, July 2005. https://www.dvidshub.net/news/printable/2431
   - SOF Tactical Tourniquet "is being issued to all Soldiers serving in Iraq and Afghanistan"; available since January; designed for one-handed application so a soldier can apply it to himself; (the "under fifteen seconds" figure is the maker's website, quoted in the story — NOT used); handle twisted to tighten, then latched.
6. Eastridge BJ, Mabry RL, Seguin P, et al. "Death on the battlefield (2001–2011): Implications for the future of combat casualty care." J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2012;73(6 Suppl 5):S431–7. https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/death-on-the-battlefield-2001-2011-implications-for-the-future-of/
   - 4,596 battlefield fatalities; 87.3% of injury mortality occurred before reaching a medical treatment facility; 24.3% of those potentially survivable; 90.9% of potentially survivable deaths associated with hemorrhage.
7. Wall PL, Buising CM, Sahr SM. "Review: Getting Tourniquets Right = Getting Tourniquets Tight." Journal of Special Operations Medicine 2019;19(3). https://jsomonline.org/?p=12388
   - Tourniquet use "is conceptually simple, but optimal application technique matters" (usually needs training); CAT windlass; arterial occlusion pressures of "250mmHg to 428mmHg on normotensive adult thighs."
8. STOP THE BLEED — Our Story (American College of Surgeons). https://www.stopthebleed.org/our-story/
   - Sandy Hook 2012 → Dr. Lenworth M. Jacobs Jr. convened experts → Hartford Consensus (improve survival after mass shootings and other intentional mass-violence events by training bystanders). Launched October 2015 by the White House. Led by the ACS Committee on Trauma; licensing agreement with the Department of Defense.
9. News-Medical, "Stop the Bleed program surpasses one million mark…" (Sept. 8, 2019, citing ACS). https://www.news-medical.net/news/20190908/Stop-the-Bleed-program-surpasses-one-million-mark-in-training-people-to-control-major-bleeding.aspx
   - 1,025,934 people trained as of late August 2019 (ACS Committee on Trauma).
10. "They're simple and cheap, but do tourniquets save lives after mass shootings?" STAT via PBS NewsHour, June 25, 2016. https://www.pbs.org/newshour/health/theyre-simple-and-cheap-but-do-tourniquets-save-lives-after-mass-shootings
   - Boston Marathon bombing: "of 27 tourniquets put into use, all were makeshift devices, not commercially made ones, and they didn't work well." King: "They were improvised and rarely effective."
11. King DR, Larentzakis A, Ramly EP, et al. "Tourniquet use at the Boston Marathon bombing: Lost in translation." J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2015;78(3):594–9 (ASPR TRACIE summary). https://asprtracie.hhs.gov/technical-resources/resource/458/tourniquet-use-at-the-boston-marathon-bombing-lost-in-translation
12. Esmarch F. von. "Early Aid in Injuries and Accidents" (London, 1883; translated by Princess Christian), pp. 58–59, Figs. 10–11 — NLM copy on Internet Archive. https://archive.org/details/29420280R.nlm.nih.gov
   - Elastic bandage wound several times round the limb; with only a handkerchief: "put it loosely round the limb, tie the ends together, shove a short stick under it (a walking-stick, door-key, branch of a tree, a sword in its sheath, or a ramrod, will do) and twist this round and round till the bleeding stops (Fig. 11)."
