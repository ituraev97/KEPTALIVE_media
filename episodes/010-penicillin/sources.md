# Sources — KEPT ALIVE · FIRST 010 · Penicillin (publish 2026-11-11 ET)
All pages opened during research on 2026-11-11. Facts as extracted (quotes in "…").

## Discovery (1928–1929)
- **AAI — Alexander Fleming (American Association of Immunologists), https://www.aai.org/About/History/Notable-Members/Nobel-Laureates/AlexanderFleming**
  - WWI: "served as a captain in the Royal Army Medical Corps", field laboratory in France; with Wright found "the antiseptics used by surgeons in the field were more harmful than helpful", "often more effective at killing the body's infection-fighting leukocytes than infection-causing bacteria."
  - 1928: after vacation, "one of the staphylococcus cultures he had left out was contaminated by fungus"; "a ring had formed around the fungus in which staphylococci did not grow."
  - Published in the "British Journal of Experimental Pathology" in 1929; paper "largely ignored."
  - Shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Chain and Florey.
  - (Lysozyme 1921: Science History Institute page summary says Fleming discovered lysozyme in 1921.)
- **PBS NewsHour — The real story behind the world's first antibiotic, https://pbs.org/newshour/health/the-real-story-behind-the-worlds-first-antibiotic**
  - September 1928, St. Mary's Hospital, London; "returned from a summer vacation in Scotland to find a messy lab bench"; Penicillium notatum contaminated Petri dishes of Staphylococcus aureus.
  - Quote: "I certainly didn't plan to revolutionize all medicine by discovering the world's first antibiotic, or bacteria killer." (as quoted)
  - Florey read Fleming's paper (1938); Chain biochemist; Heatley developed ways to grow and purify the mold.
  - Enough penicillin for one human case took 2,000 liters of mold culture fluid.
  - First U.S. civilian: March 1942, a patient near death at New Haven Hospital, Connecticut, "first civilian patient successfully treated with penicillin" (name not used on air).
  - Jan–May 1942: 400 million units of pure penicillin manufactured; end of WWII: 650 billion units a month.
  - Bacterial pneumonia death rate 18% in WWI, less than 1% in WWII.
  - Fleming, Florey, Chain shared 1945 Nobel; Heatley excluded; Oxford honorary doctorate of medicine 1990.
  - Myth: Fleming alone developed penicillin — he did little work on it after 1928; Florey, Chain and Heatley made it usable; the Oxford group's contributions were largely ignored in early publicity.
  - NOTE: PBS dates the policeman's treatment to Sept. 1940 — conflicts with Science History Institute (February 1941, the accepted date). We use February 1941.
- **National WWII Museum — Penicillin fact sheet, https://www.nationalww2museum.org/sites/default/files/2017-07/penicillin-fact-sheet.pdf**
  - 1928 Fleming, St. Mary's; mold "blue-green"; Penicillium notatum. Fleming kept growing the strain for twelve years.
  - Disease caused "approximately one third of military deaths during the Great War."
  - 1938 Florey and Chain; May 25, 1940 Florey's team cured infected mice.
  - July 1941 Florey and Heatley to the U.S.; four companies (Merck, E. R. Squibb & Sons, Charles Pfizer & Co., Lederle); after Pearl Harbor 21 companies combined efforts; best strain from an over-ripe cantaloupe in Peoria.
  - 21 companies produced 2.3 million doses of penicillin ahead of the D-Day invasion of Normandy.

## Oxford (1938–1941)
- **Science History Institute — Howard Walter Florey and Ernst Boris Chain, https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/howard-walter-florey-and-ernst-boris-chain**
  - 1938: Florey and Chain "decided to study the biochemical and biological properties of selected antibacterial substances."
  - "On August 24, 1940, Florey and Chain reported their findings in the Lancet." Report "electrified research groups around the world".
  - "In February a policeman became the first patient with an infection to be treated with penicillin." Condition "at first improved… and then relapsed"; retrieving penicillin from his urine "failed to save him"; "The penicillin supply had almost run out."
  - Early purification: "an amount just large enough to cover a fingernail" from "many gallons of mold broth".
  - "Florey and Heatley flew across the Atlantic in the beginning of July 1941"; directed to NRRL Peoria.
  - Florey page (https://www.sciencehistory.org/education/scientific-biographies/howard-walter-florey/): Heatley later worked at Merck to solve scale-up problems.
- **Smithsonian Magazine — "Penicillin girls", https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smithsonian-institution/pencillin-girls-made-one-worlds-most-life-saving-discoveries-possible-180985449/**
  - Six female technicians, "penicillin girls"; mold grown "practically everywhere, from bedpans to bathtubs."

## Peoria and production (1941–1946)
- **USDA ARS — Penicillin: opening the era of antibiotics, https://www.ars.usda.gov/midwest-area/peoria-il/national-center-for-agricultural-utilization-research/docs/penicillin-opening-the-era-of-antibiotics/**
  - "Arriving on July 14, 1941, work on the challenge began the very next day."
  - Air pumped into "corn-steep liquor (a non-alcoholic by-product of the wet milling process)" in deep vats → "faster growth and larger amounts of penicillin than the previous surface-growth method."
  - Strain on "a moldy cantaloupe from a Peoria market" yielded the most penicillin.
  - Per-dose price: "nearly priceless in 1940", $20 in July 1943, $0.55 three years later.
- **USDA ARS — The Enduring Mystery of "Moldy Mary", https://scientificdiscoveries.ars.usda.gov/tellus/stories/articles/enduring-mystery-moldy-mary**
  - Mary Kay Hunt, NRRL technician in 1943, helped collect mold specimens "from produce markets and other local sources"; acknowledged in a 1944 paper.
  - Strain NRRL 1951, "atop a cantaloupe found at a Peoria market", "the top performer and became the 'parent' of all strains used commercially."
  - Finder uncertain (Raper 1976 credited "a Peoria woman, who handed the cantaloupe to a guard"); nickname applied "by some".
- **UW–Madison CALS — D-Day invasion was bolstered by UW–Madison penicillin project, https://news.cals.wisc.edu/2017/06/05/d-day-invasion-was-bolstered-by-uw-madison-penicillin-project**
  - Stauffer and Backus used ultraviolet light on McCoy's strain to isolate Q-176, "the most productive strain of the wartime program." D-Day "bolstered by millions of doses".
- **American Chemical Society release via EurekAlert — Pfizer deep-tank fermentation landmark (2008), https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/491868**
  - Pfizer converted an old Brooklyn ice plant; opened March 1, 1944; fourteen 7,500-gallon tanks; soon "producing more penicillin in one month than it had in all of 1943"; most of the penicillin that went ashore with Allied forces on D-Day came from the Brooklyn facility.

## Fleming's warning, mechanism, resistance today
- **Nobel Prize — Fleming Nobel Lecture "Penicillin", December 11, 1945, https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/1945/fleming/lecture/** (date and title; lecture text PDF not reachable from the cloud)
- **Live Science — the threat of superbugs was known from the first antibiotic, https://www.livescience.com/health/viruses-infections-disease/how-could-it-have-been-allowed-to-happen-the-threat-of-superbugs-was-known-from-the-first-antibiotic-but-weve-failed-to-stop-it**
  - Quotes Fleming's lecture: "The time may come when penicillin can be bought by anyone in the shops." … "Moral: If you use penicillin, use enough." (last line not used on air: reads as dosing advice)
  - Hypothetical in the lecture: a man underdoses himself, his microbes become resistant, his wife gets pneumonia, penicillin fails and she dies.
  - 1940: Edward Abraham and co-workers in Florey's lab trained cultured staphylococcus colonies to resist penicillin.
- **StatPearls (NCBI Bookshelf) — Penicillin, https://statpearls.com/point-of-care/26891**
  - "Penicillin functions by inhibiting the cross-linking of peptidoglycan within the cell wall"; catalyst = penicillin-binding proteins (DD-transpeptidase); β-lactam ring binds and inactivates it; wall weakens, "osmotic pressure pushes water into the cell and kills the cell"; some bacteria express β-lactamase, which degrades the β-lactam ring.
- **CDC — Antimicrobial resistance facts and stats, https://www.cdc.gov/antimicrobial-resistance/data-research/facts-stats/index.html**
  - "more than 2.8 million antimicrobial-resistant infections occur each year" in the U.S.; "More than 35,000 people die as a result, according to CDC's 2019 AR Threats Report."
- **UN News — WHO report, 13 October 2025, https://news.un.org/en/story/2025/10/1166087**
  - "one in six bacterial infections globally are resistant to standard antibiotics".
- **WHO — World AMR Awareness Week 2026, https://www.who.int/campaigns/world-amr-awareness-week/world-amr-awareness-week-2026**
  - 18–24 November 2026; "AMR is no longer a future challenge; it is a global threat."
- **DVIDS 876964 description (WRAIR, 2023)** — MRSN "serves as the primary surveillance organization for antibiotic-resistant bacteria across the Army, Navy and Air Force."
