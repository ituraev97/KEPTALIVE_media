# Sources — OUTBREAK 012 · Cholera and the Broad Street pump (publish 2026-11-14)
Every fact below was read on the opened page/document on 2026-11-13.

## Primary source
1. John Snow, *On the Mode of Communication of Cholera*, 2nd ed., London: John Churchill, 1855. Wellcome Collection scan, Internet Archive item b28985266 (Public Domain Mark): https://archive.org/details/b28985266 — full text read via pdftotext.
   - p. 38: "The most terrible outbreak of cholera which ever occurred in this kingdom, is probably that which took place in Broad Street, Golden Square… Within two hundred and fifty yards of the spot where Cambridge Street joins Broad Street, there were upwards of five hundred fatal attacks of cholera in ten days."
   - p. 39: water "in the form of small white, flocculent particles" over "the next two days"; list from the General Register Office: 89 deaths registered in the week ending 2 Sept.; "I made inquiry, in detail, respecting the eighty-three deaths registered as having taken place during the last three days of the week"; "nearly all the deaths had taken place within a short distance of the pump."
   - p. 39–40: "only ten deaths in houses situated decidedly nearer to another street pump. In five of these cases the families… always sent to the pump in Broad Street, as they preferred the water… In three other cases, the deceased were children who went to school near the pump… The other two deaths… represent only the amount of mortality from cholera that was occurring before the irruption took place."
   - p. 40: "sixty-one instances in which I was informed that the deceased persons used to drink the pump-water from Broad Street, either constantly or occasionally."
   - p. 40: "I had an interview with the Board of Guardians of St. James's parish, on the evening of Thursday, 7th September… In consequence of what I said, the handle of the pump was removed on the following day."
   - p. 42: Poland Street workhouse — "out of five hundred and thirty-five inmates only five died of cholera"; "more than three-fourths surrounded by houses in which deaths from cholera occurred"; "The workhouse has a pump-well on the premises… the inmates never sent to Broad Street for water."
   - p. 42–43: Brewery in Broad Street near the pump — proprietor Mr. Huggins: "above seventy workmen… none of them had suffered from cholera, — at least in a severe form"; "The men are allowed a certain quantity of malt liquor… he is quite certain that the workmen never obtained water from the pump in the street. There is a deep well in the brewery."
   - p. 44–45: Hampstead West End: the widow (aged 59; NOT named in the video) "had not been in the neighbourhood of Broad Street for many months. A cart went from Broad Street to West End every day, and it was the custom to take out a large bottle of the water from the pump in Broad Street, as she preferred it." Died 2 Sept. "A niece, who was on a visit to this lady, also drank of the water; she returned to her residence, in a high and healthy part of Islington, was attacked with cholera, and died also. There was no cholera at the time, either at West End or in the neighbourhood where the niece died."
   - p. 61: "The greatest number of attacks in any one day occurred on the 1st of September… The following day the attacks fell from one hundred and forty-three to one hundred and sixteen… On September the 8th — the day when the handle of the pump was removed — there were twelve attacks"; "the mortality was much diminished… by the flight of the population… but the attacks had so far diminished before the use of the water was stopped, that it is impossible to decide whether the well still contained the cholera poison in an active state."
   - p. 68 (OCR "185S"): "The Lambeth Company removed their water works, in 1852, from opposite Hungerford Market to Thames Ditton; thus obtaining a supply of water quite free from the sewage of London"; pipes of both companies "going down every street" in the mixed districts.
   - p. ~74: Southwark and Vauxhall Company "derived their supply from the Thames at Battersea Fields".
   - p. 75: "Each Company supplies both rich and poor, both large houses and small; there is no difference either in the condition or occupation of the persons receiving the water of the different Companies." "The experiment, too, was on the grandest scale. No fewer than three hundred thousand people… were divided into two groups without their choice, and, in most cases, without their knowledge."
   - p. 86, Table IX ("the first seven weeks of the epidemic", Table VIII = seven weeks ending 26 August 1854): Southwark & Vauxhall 40,046 houses, 1,263 deaths, 315 per 10,000 houses; Lambeth 26,107 houses, 98 deaths, 37; rest of London 59. "The mortality in the houses supplied by the Southwark and Vauxhall Company was therefore between eight and nine times as great as in the houses supplied by the Lambeth Company."
   - Map 1 (Broad Street, bars = deaths; drawn by C. F. Cheffins) and Map 2 (south London water companies: blue Southwark & Vauxhall, red Lambeth, purple intermingled) are fold-out plates of this edition.

## Snow, Broad Street, Whitehead
2. UCLA Fielding School of Public Health, John Snow archive — Broad Street pump outbreak (Judith Summers overview + Snow excerpts): https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_a.html — outbreak began night of 31 Aug 1854; "within a week, about three-quarters of residents had fled"; Board of Health report dismissed Snow's theory; Rev. Henry Whitehead of St Luke's, Berwick Street; index case at 40 Broad Street; leaking cesspool <3 ft from the well; ~70 brewery workers; 5 workhouse cases among ~530.
3. UCLA John Snow archive — Removal of handle, index case and Rev. Whitehead: https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_e.html — 5-month-old (name NOT used) at 40 Broad Street died 2 Sept 1854; mother soaked soiled diapers and emptied the water into the front cesspool; surveyor's report 1 May 1855: 2 ft 8 in from cesspool to well, decayed brickwork let drain fluid seep into the well; Whitehead first expected his inquiry to refute Snow, interviewed residents (some households 4–5 times), June 1855 report: "Slowly and I may add reluctantly"; Medical/parish committee 9 Aug 1855: outbreak "in some manner attributable to the use of the impure water of the well in Broad Street", vote split evenly, chairman's deciding vote; replica of the handle-free pump at the John Snow pub. Whitehead found the index case in April (spring) 1855.
4. UCLA John Snow archive — Map, myth and error-making: https://epi-snow.ph.ucla.edu/Stream2_BSPoutbreak_f.html — Snow's suspicion came from earlier studies, not the map; he never cited a map as the means of discovery; 3 Sept 1854 he inspected the pump (water looked clear); first showed a spot map 4 Dec 1854 at the Epidemiological Society; map in the 2nd edition of his monograph (1855).
5. London Museum, "John Snow: Cholera & the Broad Street pump": https://www.londonmuseum.org.uk/collections/london-stories/john-snow-cholera-broad-street-pump/ — epidemic began in India 1817; cholera reached Britain 1831, first epidemic to 1832 killed around 30,000; miasma = disease spread by breathing dirty air; 1849 Snow published his water theory, mostly ignored; Soho outbreak 31 Aug 1854, ~500 died in 10 days; Broad Street now Broadwick Street; Snow born 15 March 1813, died 16 June 1858 (age 45); in the 1850s Snow gave chloroform to Queen Victoria during the births of two of her children; 1858 Great Stink → modern sewer system overseen by Joseph Bazalgette; final London outbreak 1866 in east London areas fed by the Old Ford Reservoir, not connected to Bazalgette's sewers.

## The germ
6. Robert Koch Institute, "Robert Koch: One of the founders of microbiology": https://rki.de/EN/Content/Institute/History/rk_node_en.html — end of 1883 Koch and team travelled to Calcutta to study an outbreak; beginning of 1884 identified Vibrio cholerae; Filippo Pacini "had already seen and described the pathogen under a microscope back in 1854."

## Cholera today (WHO, CDC)
7. WHO fact sheet "Cholera" (29 Sept 2026): https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cholera — spread through food or water contaminated with Vibrio cholerae; most infected people do not develop symptoms; researchers estimate 1.3–4.0 million cases and 21,000–143,000 deaths per year; 2025: 47 countries reported 451,499 cases and 7,870 deaths, reported deaths highest since 1999; access to safe water, basic sanitation and hygiene (WASH) is essential to prevent cholera; seventh pandemic since 1961.
8. CDC, "About Cholera in the United States": https://www.cdc.gov/cholera/about/about-cholera-in-the-united-states.html — "Cholera is rare in the United States"; prevalent in the 1800s; "With modern water and sewer treatment systems, there are typically fewer than 20 U.S. cholera cases a year", mostly linked to travel.
9. CDC (archived), "History of Drinking Water Treatment": https://archive.cdc.gov/www_cdc_gov/healthywater/drinking/history.html — 1908 Jersey City, New Jersey, first U.S. city to begin routine disinfection of community drinking water; thousands of cities and towns followed over the next decade; cholera and typhoid "dropped dramatically"; typhoid ~100 per 100,000 in 1900, 33.8 in 1920, 0.1 in 2006 (353 cases); decline credited to disinfection and treatment, better source water and sanitation/hygiene.

## New York 1832
10. Daniel S. Levy, "How New York Survived the 1832 Cholera Epidemic", History News Network, Feb. 13, 2022: https://www.historynewsnetwork.org/article/new-york-survived-the-1832-cholera-epidemic — city of more than 200,000; about 100,000 (half) fled; 3,513 deaths by late August 1832.
