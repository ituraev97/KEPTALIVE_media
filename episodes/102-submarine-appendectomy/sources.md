# 102 — No Doctor Aboard: the submarine appendectomy (USS Seadragon, Sept. 11, 1942) — sources
All pages opened on 2026-11-17 (UTC). Patient is never named on screen or in narration (bible rule).

## Primary / official
1. Naval History and Heritage Command (NHHC), "PhM1/c Wheeler B. Lipes and a Submerged Appendectomy" — https://history.navy.mil/browse-by-topic/wars-conflicts-and-operations/world-war-ii/world-war-ii-profiles/submerged-appendectomy.html
   - Symptoms first reported 8 Sept 1942; diagnosis and surgery 11 Sept 1942; Seadragon (SS-194), fourth war patrol, several days and thousands of miles from Fremantle, off the Indonesian coast; crew 55.
   - Lipes PhM1/c, age 23; joined Navy 1936; Navy hospital course San Diego; naval hospitals Philadelphia and Canacao (near Manila); submarines 1941; classified as electro-cardiographer; had assisted in several appendectomies.
   - Patient: seaman, age 19 (named on the page — NOT named by us). CO Lt. Cmdr. William E. Ferrall; anesthetist Lt. Franz Hoskins, communications officer.
   - Ferrall asked if Lipes could do it: "Yes sir, I can do it," … "everything is against us." Patient: "Whatever the doc feels has to be done is okay with me." Ferrall ordered him to operate.
   - Took the boat into relatively safe water, submerged to 120 feet. Ether 1046, incision 1107, suturing finished 1322. CO recorded observations at intervals (page: 4–7 min; report: 1–4 min → we say "every few minutes").
   - Hemostat as scalpel handle; five tablespoons with bent-back handles as retractors; "Handi-pads" for gauze sponges; gauze-covered tea strainer as ether mask; boiling water and torpedo alcohol for sterilization; wardroom table as operating table. Kit: sulfa tablets, twelve hemostats, scalpel blades, catgut, limited ether.
   - Sulfa tablets ground to powder, baked in the ship's oven, sprinkled in the abdominal cavity. Distal tip of appendix black and gangrenous; preserved in torpedo alcohol; stump cauterized with carbolic acid.
   - Three-inch incision healed; back on duty within a few days; Seadragon returned to port six weeks after the operation; squadron medical officer declared him fit and concluded the crew had saved his life.
2. Navy Department Library, Submarine Squadron Two report to the Bureau of Medicine and Surgery, 22 Oct 1942 — https://history.navy.mil/research/library/oral-histories/wwii/oral-history-appendectomy-performed-on-fourth-war-patrol-of-ussseadragon-1942/submarine-squadron-two-report-appendectomy.html
   - 120 feet; internal pressure two inches of mercury above atmospheric; anesthesia 1046, incision 1107, appendix amputated 1230, closure 1322 (2 h 36 min ether→closure).
   - Pocket case of twelve hemostats, scalpel blades (hemostat as handle), five tablespoons with bent handles; No. 0 twenty-day chromic catgut; instruments boiled; gloves and pajamas sterilized in alcohol; commercially sterilized Handi-pads; ward-room mess table; ether via gauze-covered tea strainer; sulfanilamide powder in the peritoneal cavity and on each closure layer.
   - Operator Lipes, Wheeler B., PhM1c; anesthesia Lt. F. P. Hoskins, USNR; CO Lt. Cdr. W. E. Ferrall recorded observations.
   - Highest temperature 103.6 at the 18th post-operative hour; oxygen during post-operative depression with marked improvement; water by mouth six hours after consciousness returned; convalescence after day four uneventful; six weeks later the squadron Medical Officer found a firm, non-tender scar.
   - Medical Officer: major surgery by non-surgeons "by no means desirable to encourage"; result "entirely satisfactory".
   - NOTE: the web page header mislabels the boat ("Sargo") and has a stray "1999" date; we use only facts that match source 1. Time to regain consciousness differs between sources (report 2 h 52 min vs. Weller "about half an hour") → we do not state it.
3. Navy Department Library, "Oral history: appendectomy performed on fourth war patrol of USS Seadragon, 1942" (Lipes's recollections, adapted from U.S. Navy Medicine, 1987) — https://history.navy.mil/research/library/oral-histories/wwii/oral-history-appendectomy-performed-on-fourth-war-patrol-of-ussseadragon-1942.html
   - Lipes at Cavite when bombers struck, 10 Dec 1941; patient found in the after battery (crew's compartment): "Hey Doc, I don't feel very good."
   - Captain: "I fire torpedoes every day and some of them miss." Lipes: he "could not fire this torpedo and miss."
   - 3-inch incision; appendix gangrenous ~two-thirds along; cauterized with phenol; sulfa tablets ground and heated to kill spores.
   - Cook: "Doc, you must have sewed him up with rubber bands, the way he eats." Back on duty within days.
   - Met Admiral Lockwood and reporter George Weller in the wardroom of the submarine tender USS Holland after the patrol. Returned to the States January 1943.
   - The patient died 24 Oct 1944 when USS Tang was sunk; nine survivors. Lipes recalled that his grandson's teacher dismissed the story.
4. NHHC, DANFS "Seadragon I (SS-194)" — https://history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/s/seadragon-i.html
   - Commissioned 23 Oct 1939. Cavite 10 Dec 1941: moored with Sealion; Sealion took a direct hit; explosion ripped off part of Seadragon's bridge; shrapnel pierced conning tower, killed one man, wounded five.
   - Fourth patrol: left Australian base 26 Aug 1942; station 12 Sept, steamer lanes west of Macclesfield Bank off Indochina; 11 Sept progress "delayed by an emergency appendectomy performed successfully by the pharmacist's mate". 22 Sept attack on a cruiser, no hits; 30 Sept surface attack damaged one ship of a convoy; 10 Oct sank Shigitre Maru 47 seconds after the first torpedo explosion.
   - 12 war patrols, 11 battle stars; credited sinkings include Japanese submarine I-4.
5. NHHC, DANFS "Grayback I (SS-208)" — https://history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/g/grayback-i.html
   - Fifth patrol sailed from Australia 7 Dec 1942; "only a week out of port" PhM1c Harry Roby removed a torpedoman's appendix "a hundred feet beneath the surface in the Bismarck Archipelago"; patient back on watch by end of patrol (patient not named by us).
   - Listed overdue, presumed lost 30 March 1944.
6. NHHC, DANFS "Silversides I (SS-236)" — https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/s/silversides-i.html
   - 10 May 1942 an enemy machine-gun bullet killed one of her deck gunners. Christmas Eve (1942, fourth patrol): the pharmacist's mate performed a successful emergency appendectomy, "the operation over at 0400 on the 25th". (Page does not name the pharmacist's mate or say the boat was submerged → we don't.)
7. BUMED / DVIDS, Matt Lyman, "A brief history of the US Navy Independent Duty Corpsman" (5 Dec 2014) — https://www.dvidshub.net/news/printable/149637
   - 1944 Navy identified need for medical personnel where no medical officer available → IDCs; 1944 intermediate course at Hospital Corps School Portsmouth, VA: 245 h classroom + 235 h practical; separate courses for submarine IDCs (Groton, CT) and surface (San Diego). 1949 → 20-week advanced course San Diego; a year later 26 weeks. Today courses in North Carolina, Connecticut, California; submarine IDCs complete part of Basic Enlisted Submarine School before getting underway.
8. Navy Medicine, André B. Sobocinski (historian), "An undersea medical officer's tale from the Silent Service" (20 June 2024) — https://www.med.navy.mil/Media/News/Article/3811813/an-undersea-medical-officers-tale-from-the-silent-service/
   - Corpsmen aboard submarines since 1924; IDCs provide most daily medical care. 1959 into the 1970s SSBN Blue and Gold crews each had a physician and an IDC; BUMED-sponsored report found IDCs could handle most submarine medical services. Capt. Schwartz set a fracture, applied a cast, boat stayed on patrol with about two or three weeks remaining.
9. Navy Medicine, PO2 Victoria Granado, "Independent Duty Corpsman: A Department of One" (16 Mar 2023) — https://www.med.navy.mil/Media/News/Article/3332617/independent-duty-corpsman-a-department-of-one/
   - HM1 Seo Song, IDC, USS Mobile (LCS 26): only corpsman aboard, sole medical provider underway; crew of more than 100; sick call, medical training, daily water testing, bi-weekly pest surveys; reports directly to CO and XO. Quote: "The work is stressful because all of the responsibility and accountability lies completely with me."
10. NIDDK (NIH), "Definition & Facts for Appendicitis" — https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/appendicitis/definition-facts
   - Inflammation of the appendix, a finger-like pouch attached to the large intestine; most common cause of acute abdominal pain requiring surgery in the U.S.; about 5 to 9 of every 100 people develop it; untreated appendicitis can cause the appendix to burst; peritonitis is a serious infection that can spread in the abdomen.

## Press / secondary
11. NPR, "Navy Hero to Earn Medal for 1942 Surgery at Sea" (19 Feb 2005) — https://www.npr.org/2005/02/19/4503760/navy-hero-to-earn-medal-for-1942-surgery-at-sea
   - Navy Commendation Medal on Sunday Feb. 20 (2005), more than 60 years after the surgery, for saving a crewman's life; Lipes 84; Weller's Chicago Daily News story won a Pulitzer; patient on waking: "I'm still in there pitching"; Lipes: "It wasn't one of those 'snappy valve' appendixes."
12. Pulitzer Prizes, "George Weller of Chicago Daily News" — https://www.pulitzer.org/winners/george-weller
   - Citation: "…performed an operation for appendicitis saving a sailor's life" (listed with 1943 prize winners in Reporting).
13. WW2 on Deadline (George Weller archive, Substack), "Surgery under the sea: George Weller's Pulitzer-winning thriller" — https://ww2ondeadline.substack.com/p/george-weller-correspondent-pulitzer-prize
   - Published 14 Dec 1942 (Chicago Daily News); 1943 Pulitzer Prize for Reporting; Lipes, 23, a lab technician who had worked three years in a Philadelphia hospital; others waited in the after torpedo room; "the first appendix ever known to have been removed below enemy waters"; tea-strainer ether mask; spoons; alcohol from the torpedoes; patient's first words "I'm still in there pitching."
14. Wikipedia, "Wheeler Bryson Lipes" (cites Washington Post obituary 2005, NPR 2005, Herman 1997 "Battle Station Sick Bay") — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheeler_Bryson_Lipes
   - Born 12 July 1920, New Castle, Virginia; died 17 April 2005, New Bern, NC, aged 84. Medical Service Corps commission 1951; served 1936–1962; retired as lieutenant commander; retired 1991 as president of Memorial Medical Center, Corpus Christi, Texas. Navy Commendation Medal, Feb 2005, ceremony at Camp Lejeune, NC. "Destination Tokyo" (1943, Cary Grant) incorporated the events; Cavalcade of America radio "Pharmacist's Mate, First Class", 24 May 1943, Lipes appeared at the end; The Silent Service TV episode "Operation Seadragon", 1958. Weller won the 1943 Pulitzer Prize for Reporting for his Dec. 14, 1942 article.
