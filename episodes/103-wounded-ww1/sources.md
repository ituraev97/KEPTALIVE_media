## 1. AEF casualty numbers

1. Official DoD total for WWI: 4,734,991 served, 53,402 battle deaths, 63,114 other deaths, 204,002 wounds not mortal — "53,402" / "204,002" — [CRS RL32492, American War and Military Operations Casualties (2019)](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190924_RL32492_75dc0f1f826404a4728a7a3eb43ec2f8cb67905d.html)
2. The **Army** alone: 4,057,101 served, 50,510 battle deaths, 55,868 other deaths, 193,663 wounded (Marines: 2,461 battle deaths, 9,520 wounded) — [CRS RL32492](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190924_RL32492_75dc0f1f826404a4728a7a3eb43ec2f8cb67905d.html)
3. NPS: "The United States suffered 53,402 battle deaths in World War I, in less than six months of fighting." "Another 63,114 died from accidents and disease." — [NPS, The United States and the First World War](https://www.nps.gov/wwim/wwioverview.htm)
4. KUMC gives slightly different totals: killed "52,947", wounded "202,628" — [KUMC, American Military Operations and Casualties](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/american-military-operations-and-casualties.html)
5. Period figures (Ayres, *The War with Germany: A Statistical Summary*, 1919): killed in action 34,180; died of wounds 14,729; wounded 230,074 (80,130 severely, 110,544 slightly, 39,400 undetermined) — [Ayres 1919, Ch. IX Health and Casualties (VIU transcription)](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H482.WWI/Ayer.stats.WWI.htm)
6. Ayres 1919: "The number who died of wounds was only 6 per cent as large as the number who were wounded." — [Ayres 1919](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H482.WWI/Ayer.stats.WWI.htm)
7. Ayres 1919: "For every man who was killed in battle, seven others were wounded, taken prisoner, or reported missing." — [Ayres 1919](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H482.WWI/Ayer.stats.WWI.htm)
8. Ayres 1919: "Five out of every six men sent to hospitals on account of wounds were cured and returned to duty." — [Ayres 1919](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H482.WWI/Ayer.stats.WWI.htm)
9. Ayres 1919: "Pneumonia killed more soldiers than were killed in battle." (This compares with killed in action only, not died of wounds.) — [Ayres 1919](https://web.viu.ca/davies/H482.WWI/Ayer.stats.WWI.htm)
10. Heller, citing the Medical Department statistics: 34,249 immediate battlefield deaths; 224,089 wounded evacuated to medical facilities, of whom 70,552 were gassed — [Heller, Leavenworth Paper 10, p. 91 (DTIC)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
11. Influenza and pneumonia: "about 45,000 American Soldiers died of influenza and related pneumonia by the end of 1918." — [National Guard, Flu outbreak killed 45,000 US soldiers during WWI](https://www.nationalguard.mil/News/Article-View/Article/1616713/flu-outbreak-killed-45000-us-soldiers-during-world-war-i)
12. In France in 1918, doctors "hospitalized 340,000 Soldiers for influenza" while "227,000 Soldiers were in the hospital due to wounds suffered in battle." — [National Guard](https://www.nationalguard.mil/News/Article-View/Article/1616713/flu-outbreak-killed-45000-us-soldiers-during-world-war-i). Byerly gives the same figures — [Byerly, Public Health Reports 2010 (PMC2862337)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2862337)
13. During the Meuse-Argonne in October 1918, "1,451 Americans died from the flu." — [National Guard](https://www.nationalguard.mil/News/Article-View/Article/1616713/flu-outbreak-killed-45000-us-soldiers-during-world-war-i)
14. Influenza sickened 26% of the Army (more than 1 million men) and killed almost 30,000 before they ever reached France (the War Department's most conservative count). Influenza "clogged" evacuation lines during the Meuse-Argonne — [Byerly, "The U.S. Military and the Influenza Pandemic of 1918–1919," Public Health Reports 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2862337)
15. Army Medical Department strength: 30,500 medical officers, 21,500 nurses, 264,000 enlisted men; Army hospital capacity in the U.S. grew from 9,500 to 120,000 beds — [Byerly 2010](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2862337)
16. Gas: "In the A.E.F., 70,522 cases related to gas exposure were reported, 31.49 per cent of all casualties." "1,221 died, the majority from mustard gas exposure." — [Army Heritage Center Foundation, Gas Warfare in World War I](https://www.armyheritage.org/soldier-stories-information/gas-warfare-in-world-war-i/)
17. Heller: gas caused 27.3% of all AEF casualties (dead and wounded) and 31.4% of wounded treated in hospitals; about 200 battlefield gas deaths ("a rough and perhaps low estimate"); 1,221 deaths in hospital — [Heller p. 91](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
18. Mustard gas caused 39% of AEF gas casualties — [Heller p. 67](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
19. First German gas projector attack on U.S. troops: 26 Feb 1918, Ansauville sector, 1st Division. Of 225 men exposed, 85 became casualties and 8 died (2 at once, 6 after reaching the field hospital) — [Heller p. 76](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
20. Pershing on gas: "the effect is so deadly to the unprepared that we can never afford to neglect the question." — [Heller p. 91](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
21. Meuse-Argonne: began 26 Sept 1918 at 5:30 a.m. after a 3-hour bombardment and lasted 47 days. "Almost 1.25 million American troops" took part. "American casualties were high -- over 117,000" — [Army.mil, Final Allied offensive of World War I decided fate of Europe](https://www.army.mil/article/212491)
22. LOC: "26,277 Americans died" in the Meuse-Argonne, "the highest American death toll for any single battle in U.S. history." The opening force was more than 600,000 American and French troops, with almost 5,000 guns, 500 tanks and 500 airplanes — [Library of Congress, WWI American Experiences: Meuse-Argonne](https://www.loc.gov/exhibitions/world-war-i-american-experiences/about-this-exhibition/over-there/meuse-argonne/)
23. NPS: "Over 47 days, 1.2 million American troops drove the Germans back 40 miles…" "More than 26,000 American soldiers died." — [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/wwim/wwioverview.htm)
24. KUMC lists Meuse-Argonne (26 Sept–11 Nov 1918) casualties as 110,508 — [KUMC, American Military Operations and Casualties](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/american-military-operations-and-casualties.html)
25. The Meuse-Argonne American Cemetery holds "a total of 14,246" graves, the largest number of U.S. military dead in Europe, and lists 954 missing — [ABMC, Meuse-Argonne American Cemetery](https://abmc.gov/Meuse-Argonne)
26. First AEF combat casualty: Capt. Louis J. Genella, Medical Reserve Corps, wounded by artillery near Arras on 14 July 1917 — [ABMC, Caring for the Sick and Wounded](https://abmc.gov/news-events/news/caring-sick-and-wounded-establishing-first-us-army-medical-units-france-and-england)

**CONTRADICTIONS and SAFE ON AIR phrasing**
- Battle deaths: 53,402 (DoD/CRS, NPS) vs 52,947 (KUMC) vs 48,900 (Ayres 1919, an early count). **SAFE:** "about 53,000 American battle deaths" (cite DoD).
- Wounded: 204,002 (DoD) vs 202,628 (KUMC) vs 224,089 hospitalized wounded and gassed (Medical Department, via Heller) vs 230,074 (Ayres 1919). These counts differ because some include gas cases and men counted twice. **SAFE:** "more than 200,000 wounded".
- Disease and other deaths: 63,114 is *all* non-battle deaths, including accidents. About 45,000 of these were influenza and pneumonia by the end of 1918 (National Guard). **SAFE:** "flu and pneumonia killed roughly as many soldiers as enemy fire" or "about 45,000 soldiers died of flu and pneumonia". Don't say "more died of flu than combat" without the qualifier; Byerly says that only for soldiers *and* sailors combined.
- Gas: 70,552 (Heller table and Medical Department) vs 70,522 (AHCF, almost certainly a transposition). Gas share: 31.4% or 31.5% of hospitalized wounded, 27.3% of all casualties. **SAFE:** "about 70,000 gassed — nearly a third of Americans treated for wounds — but only about 1,200 of them died in hospital."
- Meuse-Argonne casualties: 110,508 (KUMC) vs "over 117,000" (Army.mil); deaths 26,277 (LOC). **SAFE:** "about 26,000 killed and more than 110,000 casualties".

## 2. What wounded men

27. Hospitalized AEF casualties by agent (Medical Department figures, Heller Table 2): gun (miscellaneous) 74,883 (33.4%); war gases 70,552 (31.5%); shrapnel 33,787 (15.1%); rifle bullets 20,420 (9.1%); shells 17,439 (7.8%); aerial bombs 3,148; grenades 880 (0.4%); pistol bullets 242; bayonets 235 (0.1%); unknown 2,491 — [Heller Table 2, p. 92](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
28. Hospital deaths by agent (same table): gun (misc.) 7,474; shrapnel 1,985; shells 1,703; war gases 1,221; rifle bullets 961 — [Heller p. 92](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
29. Bayonet wounds were rare: 235 bayonet cases, 0.1% of hospitalized casualties — [Heller p. 92](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/ADA189331.pdf)
30. Army Borden Institute textbook: small arms "were responsible for only 14% of American casualties"; Table 2-3 shows fragments (shells, grenades, mortar bombs) at about 85% for the U.S. column. The extracted table was garbled, so check the 85% reading against the printed table — [Bellamy & Zajtchuk, Conventional Warfare, Ch. 2 (Borden Institute)](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/borden/conventional-warfare/ch02.pdf)
31. National WWI Museum: 60% of WWI battlefield casualties (all armies) came from exploding artillery shells — [National WWI Museum, Artillery](https://www.theworldwar.org/learn/about-wwi/artillery)
32. Sgt. Donald Kyler (AEF) on his own company: shell-fragment wounds were "the most numerous and the most serious of any suffered by my company." — [AHCF, Kyler on Shell Frags](https://www.armyheritage.org/images/stories/Education_Images/Kyler/Kyler_on_Shell_Frags.pdf)
33. NLM / *Medical Dept. in the World War* Vol. XIV (1926): "The extensive use of poisonous gases was one of the most important developments of the World War" — [NLM Circulating Now, "Gassed!"](https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2017/10/04/gassed)

**CAUTION:** The Medical Department category "gun (misc.)" is ambiguous; it most likely means gunshot or missile wounds with the weapon not specified. Don't combine it with "rifle bullets" on air. **SAFE:** "Artillery and gas, not bullets, filled most American hospital beds. Bayonet wounds were about one in a thousand."

## 3. The chain of evacuation

### Front line: first-aid packet, aid men, litter bearers
34. Every soldier carried a first-aid packet: two gauze bandages (4" x 84"), two compresses (3½" x 3½") and two safety pins, and was taught by the battalion surgeon to apply it — [KUMC, Regimental Aid Station](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html)
35. Each infantry company had 2 Medical Department enlisted men (8 per battalion) who found, marked and gathered the wounded. About 40% of wounded could walk to the rear; litter bearers carried the rest — [KUMC, Regimental Aid Station](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html)
36. Stretcher-bearers had a "ten-week course," worked "in teams of four to six," and went over the top unarmed, often through mud "at times up to their knees." — [KUMC, Stretcher-Bearers](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/stretcher-bearers.html)
37. Wooden litter handles rotted and splintered; bearers' hands were covered in blisters and splinters. They drew trench maps and updated them as trenches collapsed — [KUMC, Stretcher-Bearers](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/stretcher-bearers.html)

### Battalion aid station
38. Battalion aid stations sat 500–1,000 yards behind the line in a dugout or building. Each held 12–30 patients and had 1 medical officer, 4–6 enlisted men, 2 runners and litter squads — [KUMC, Regimental Aid Station](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html)
39. Care at the aid station: iodine on the wound, 500 units of anti-tetanus serum, ¼ grain of morphine, tourniquet or ligature, a Thomas splint for fractures, and blankets and hot drinks against shock — [KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html)
40. Each man got a field card in one of 8 categories, from "very slightly wounded" to gassed, psychoneurotic or sick. Because of the risk of gas gangrene, surgery within 12 hours of wounding was considered critical. Evacuation often waited for night — [KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html)
41. Thomas splint: introduced at the front in 1916 and credited with cutting femur-fracture mortality from about 80% to 20% by 1918. These are **British** figures — [UK National Archives blog, A revolution in treatment: the Thomas splint](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/thomas-splint)

### Ambulance companies and dressing stations
42. Each division's Sanitary Train (about 950 men) had 1 horse-drawn and 3 motor ambulance companies, with 12 animal-drawn and 36 motor ambulances. The horse company was held in reserve because it was "a large target," slow, and its animals tired quickly — [KUMC, Ambulance Section](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/ambulance-section.html)
43. Advanced dressing stations were 1,500–2,000 yards from the front (1 officer, 15 men); main dressing stations 3,000–6,000 yards (up to 5 officers, 25 men). In the Argonne, wounded walked "because roads were impassable to ambulances" — [KUMC, Ambulance Section](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/ambulance-section.html)
44. Ford Model T ambulance: carried 3 litters or 4 seated patients (plus 2 beside the driver), had a 20 hp engine and was hand-cranked, and was light enough for soldiers to lift out of a shell hole. "By Nov. 1, 1918, 4,362 Model T ambulances had been shipped overseas." — [National Museum of the USAF, Ford Model T Ambulance](https://www.nationalmuseum.af.mil/Visit/Museum-Exhibits/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/197425/ford-model-t-ambulance/)
45. The White Motor Co. 1917 ambulance was one of the two most-used U.S. Army ambulances (the other was the Model T). It carried 4 litter patients, a driver and a corpsman, at about 20 mph and cost about $1,650 — [DVIDS, AMEDD Museum Model T Ambulance (caption)](https://www.dvidshub.net/image/5912226/amedd-museum-model-t-ambulance) (Note: the source does not name GMC; we found no confirmation for GMC.)
46. Roads were "constantly jammed" with divisions moving in and out of the line. Fire often made it "oftentimes impossible to remove the wounded until dark." Ambulances bogged in mud in the Meuse on 13 Oct 1918 — [AMEDD nurse history, Ch. 4 pt 1 (citing Medical Dept. Vol. VIII)](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/ATC%20ch%204%20pt%201.pdf)
47. Time to the field hospital: on average "a period between 3 to 8 hours" from wounding, despite congested roads. At the start of the Argonne drive, evacuation hospitals were 30–60 km behind the field hospitals — [AMEDD nurse history, Ch. 4 pt 1](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/ATC%20ch%204%20pt%201.pdf)

### Field hospitals and "triage"
48. Each division had 4 field hospitals (25 officers, 337 men in total), each holding 216 patients, sited 6–8 miles behind the front. One served as the "Triage", which sorted the sick and wounded and decided who could be transported — [KUMC, Triage–Field Hospital Section](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html)
49. 82nd Division at St. Mihiel: Field Hospital 328 at Dieulouard was the Triage and began receiving wounded 8 hours after the 12 Sept 1918 attack. Field Hospital 325 took the sick, 326 the gassed, and 327 the seriously wounded — [KUMC, Triage](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html)
50. Shock treatment at the field hospital included IV saline and "blood transfusion from matched donors." Gassed men were stripped and bathed. Men expected to recover within 14 days stayed with the division — [KUMC, Triage](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html)
51. "War neurosis" was sorted into six labels: shell fright, gas fright, hysteria, fatigue, malingering and cowardice. 65% were kept at division level and 35% sent to a neurological hospital — [KUMC, Triage](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html)

### Evacuation hospitals, mobile hospitals, trains
52. Evacuation hospitals were planned for 432 patients and enlarged to 1,000 (34 officers, 237 men). They stood 9–15 miles behind the front, and moving one took "ninety 3-ton trucks or 30 rail cars." — [KUMC, Evacuation Hospital](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/evacuation-hospital.html)
53. Operating room: 4 teams on 8 tables (each team 2 surgeons, 1 anesthetist, 2 nurses), up to 14 teams in a push. An experienced team did 35–40 operations a shift — [KUMC, Evacuation Hospital](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/evacuation-hospital.html)
54. AMEDD history: the Army formed 29 evacuation hospitals, each with 1,000 beds plus 500 emergency, about 50 nurses, and teams on 12-hour shifts with two tables each so one could be cleaned while the other was used — [AMEDD, Skilled and Resolute, Ch. 1 "World War I"](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/MASH%20Ch%201.pdf)
55. Hospital trains carried up to 400 patients, against 4 per ambulance. Trains were sometimes sidetracked, causing preventable deaths — [Skilled and Resolute, Ch. 1](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/MASH%20Ch%201.pdf)
56. 12th Evacuation Hospital at Royaumeix: 2,700 patients in 56 days, about 1,000 seriously wounded. Wounded kept arriving after the armistice because some could not be reached while fighting continued — [Skilled and Resolute, Ch. 1](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/MASH%20Ch%201.pdf)
57. Mobile hospitals (modelled on the French *auto-chir*) were "designed to deliver lifesaving surgery close to the front." By war's end 12 units had treated only about 1% of Army wounded. The St. Mihiel plan used 10 evacuation hospitals, 5 mobile hospitals and 9 evacuation ambulance companies for 33,000 expected casualties — [KUMC, Corps–Army Levels](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/corps-army-levels.html)
58. Period surgeon (Annals of Surgery, 1918): "the patient should arrive there within 12 hours." Men operated on within 12 hours of wounding had about 80% of wounds closed successfully within 10 days. At a British general hospital, by contrast, men arrived about 3 days after wounding — [D. F. Jones, "The Role of the Evacuation Hospital in the Care of the Wounded," Annals of Surgery 1918 (PMC10211230)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10211230/)

### Base hospitals, hospital centers, convalescence
59. Base Hospital No. 4 (Lakeside, Cleveland) left Cleveland on 6 May 1917 with 26 officers, 64 nurses and 156 enlisted men, reached Rouen on 25 May 1917, and treated 82,179 cases in 20 months. It was "the first contingent of the American expeditionary forces to be transported to Europe" — [Encyclopedia of Cleveland History (Case Western), Lakeside Unit](https://case.edu/ech/articles/l/lakeside-unit-world-war-i)
60. ABMC: Base Hospital No. 4 sailed from New York on 8 May 1917 under Maj. Harry L. Gilchrist. It "was one of six mobilized immediately to assist the British Expeditionary Force in France." — [ABMC, American Medical Units Mobilize](https://api.abmc.gov/news-events/news/remembering-world-war-i-american-medical-units-mobilize-shortly-after-us-enters-war)
61. Base Hospital No. 5 (Harvard) arrived at Camiers less than a month after a 1 May 1917 call for volunteers and treated over 45,000 British and American patients — [Harvard Medical School, Never Forgotten](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/never-forgotten)
62. 4 Sept 1917: a German air raid on Base Hospital No. 5 killed Lt. William T. Fitzsimons and Pvts. Oscar Tugo, Rudolph Rubino and Leslie Woods, the first U.S. Army deaths from enemy action. Fitzsimons was the first U.S. officer killed — [KUMC, William Fitzsimons](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/biography/william-fitzsimons.html); [HMS](https://hms.harvard.edu/news/never-forgotten)
63. Base Hospital No. 20 held up to 2,275 patients in a 500-bed facility; Base Hospital No. 10 took 1,400 patients in its first week — [ANCA, Contributions of the US ANC in WWI](https://e-anca.org/History/Topics-in-ANC-History/Contributions-of-the-US-Army-Nurse-Corps-in-WWI)
64. By the end of the war, Army facilities in France could treat 403,000 bed cases. Taking in over 1,000 patients and doing 100+ operations in a day was "not unusual" — [ABMC, Caring for the Sick and Wounded](https://abmc.gov/news-events/news/caring-sick-and-wounded-establishing-first-us-army-medical-units-france-and-england)
65. 21 hospital centers were planned (Mars-sur-Allier, Allerey, Savenay, Vichy and others). Base Hospital 69 at Savenay fitted prostheses, No. 15 at Chaumont did face cases, No. 64 took gas cases, and No. 117 was neuropsychiatric — [KUMC, Specialized Hospitals](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/specialized-hospitals.html)
66. Base Hospital No. 28 at Limoges was "about 200 miles from the front." Men who could not return to service went to general hospitals in the U.S. — [KUMC, Introduction to the System](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/introduction-to-the-system.html)
67. Hospitals were rows of tents, typically 60 beds each. Stimson described "rows and rows of canvas tents, each of which holds about 14 beds." — [ABMC, Caring for the Sick and Wounded](https://abmc.gov/news-events/news/caring-sick-and-wounded-establishing-first-us-army-medical-units-france-and-england) (These numbers differ in the same source; the tent sizes varied.)

**Wound-to-surgery time, SAFE:** no single official average was found. Say: "Doctors aimed to operate within 12 hours, and most men reached a field hospital 3 to 8 hours after being hit. In a big push it could take far longer, often waiting for dark."

## 4. Army Nurse Corps and Red Cross

68. April 1917: 403 active-duty and 170 reserve Army nurses, while the Red Cross held about 8,000 reserve nurses. By war's end there were 21,480 Army nurses, more than 10,000 of them overseas — [ANCA, Contributions of the US ANC in WWI](https://e-anca.org/History/Topics-in-ANC-History/Contributions-of-the-US-Army-Nurse-Corps-in-WWI)
69. AMEDD nurse history: 21,480 served, and 10,245 ("slightly less than half") reached France — [AMEDD nurse history, Ch. 3 "Stateside Nursing"](https://medcoeckapwstorprd01.blob.core.usgovcloudapi.net/pfw-images/dbimages/ATC%20ch%203.pdf)
70. No Army nurse died from enemy action, 3 were wounded by shellfire, and 272 died of disease (mostly TB, influenza and pneumonia) — [ANCA](https://e-anca.org/History/Topics-in-ANC-History/Contributions-of-the-US-Army-Nurse-Corps-in-WWI)

## 0. The AEF wounded soldier's journey (context and scale)

1. Every soldier carried his own first-aid packet: two gauze bandages (4"x84"), two compresses and two safety pins. — "protect the wound from further trauma" — [Regimental Aid Station, KUMC WWI essays](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html) [UNIV]
2. About 40% of the wounded walked to the rear; the rest were carried by regimental stretcher bearers. Battalion aid stations sat 500–1,000 yards behind the companies and held 12–30 patients. — "about 40%"; "500 to 1,000 yards" — same page [UNIV]
3. At the aid station: iodine painted around the wound, **500 units of tetanus serum**, ¼ grain of morphine, hemorrhage control, a **Thomas splint** for fractures, then blankets, a warming table and hot drinks for shock. — "500 units of tetanus serum"; "one-fourth grain of morphine" — same page [UNIV]
4. Speed mattered because gas gangrene was a threat if surgery did not happen within about 12 hours of wounding. — "12 hours" — same page [UNIV]
5. Stretcher bearers took a ten-week course and worked in teams of four to six, sometimes wading through knee-deep mud. — "they would carry a casualty through enemy fire only to find that when they finally got to an aid station or ambulance he was dead" (quote is 25+ words; trim on air) — [Stretcher Bearers, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/stretcher-bearers.html) [UNIV]
6. A US division's Field Hospital Section had four field hospitals (25 officers, 337 men), each holding 216 patients, sited 6–8 miles behind the front. AEF medical staff learned the French "triage" method in 1918. — "to sort, classify and distribute the sick and wounded" — [Triage-Field Hospital Section, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html) [UNIV, citing Med. Dept. vols VIII and XI]
7. At triage, surgery was preparatory, not definitive: control bleeding, adjust fractures, and debride only if time allowed. The mission was "to first save lives and then to prepare their patients" for the evacuation hospital. — same page [UNIV]
8. Evacuation times in 1918: at Soissons, wounded were cleared "from the battlefield within six hours"; at St. Mihiel some reached Evacuation Hospital No. 1 "within three hours of injury." — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
9. Delay killed. On 7 June 1918 many of the 700+ cases reaching Red Cross Military Hospital No. 2 in Paris had not yet been operated on, and many died on the way. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
10. AEF totals: peak 1,910,934 men in Europe (Nov 1918); **224,089 wounded, 36,694 killed in action, 13,691 died of wounds**; Medical Dept. of 18,146 officers, 10,081 nurses and 145,815 enlisted men. — AMEDD-Ch4 (citing Love, *Medical and Casualty Statistics*, vol. XV) [OFFICIAL]
    - CONTRADICTION: the Congressional Research Service gives 53,402 battle deaths and **204,002 "wounds not mortal"** for the whole US military — [CRS RL32492 via EveryCRSReport](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/20190924_RL32492_75dc0f1f826404a4728a7a3eb43ec2f8cb67905d.html) [OFFICIAL]. KUMC says "52,947 killed and 202,628 wounded" — [American Military Operations and Casualties, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/american-military-operations-and-casualties.html). The totals differ because they count different things: 224,089 includes those who later died of wounds, and 204,002 counts only non-mortal wounds. **On air:** "about 200,000 Americans were wounded," or quote CRS's 204,002 non-fatal wounds.
11. In the Meuse-Argonne alone, the Medical Department treated **72,467 wounded, 18,664 gassed and 2,029 neurological cases**. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
12. Hospital deaths rose from 4–5% of battle-injury admissions (June–Aug 1918) to over 6.6% (Oct–Nov 1918). — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]

## 1. Wound infection: gas gangrene, tetanus, debridement, Carrel–Dakin

13. Gas gangrene was caused by anaerobic *Clostridia* in soil fertilized with manure for centuries; shelling and trench digging churned them into wounds. — "almost uniformly fatal" (in that era) — [Gas Gangrene in the First World War, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/gas-gangrene.html) [UNIV]
    - Note: "almost uniformly fatal" is the essay's overstatement. The data in items 15 and 16 show 22–23% mortality in treated series. Use "often fatal."
14. Toxins caused severe pain, gaseous swelling and rapid muscle death (myonecrosis). Shrapnel carried dirt from trenches and uniforms deep into wounds. — [In Flanders Fields, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/in-flanders-fields.html) [UNIV]
15. Frances Ivens (Scottish Women's Hospital, Royaumont) studied 2,267 French soldiers from Jan 1915 to Oct 1916. Of these, **107 had gas gangrene and 25 died (23.4%)** (Proc R Soc Med, 1916). — same page [UNIV citing a peer-reviewed 1916 paper]
16. In the BEF, gas gangrene fell from 5–6% of all wounds in 1915 to just over 1% in 1918, with 22% mortality (Butler, *Official History of the Australian Army Medical Services*, vol. II). — [Scotland, "Disaster to Triumph", *Stand To!* No. 122 (Western Front Association)](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf) [SECONDARY citing official history]
17. A French anti-gas-gangrene serum existed. — "probably of little effectiveness" — [Gas Gangrene, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/gas-gangrene.html) [UNIV]
18. Belgian surgeon Antoine Depage revived **debridement**: cutting away dead tissue until healthy muscle was reached. — "any dead tissue left in the wound would impede healing" — same page [UNIV]
19. Wound excision was first published in 1915 by Henry Gray (*JRAMC*, June 1915) and Edward Milligan (*BMJ* 1915). — [Scotland, *Stand To!*](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf) [SECONDARY]
20. "Delayed primary suture" was established by 1915: the wound was left open and closed about 5 days after excision. — [Ellis, "War surgery 100 years ago", *Br J Hosp Med* 2015](https://storage.imrpress.com/IMR/hmed/application/10.12968/hmed.2015.76.7.423.pdf) [PEER]
21. US Base Hospital No. 28's lab had no proper anaerobic incubator, so it built one from a ten-gallon gasoline can. — [In Flanders Fields, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/in-flanders-fields.html) [UNIV]
22. **Tetanus, official BEF data:** per 1,000 wounded landed in England, the rate was 3.7 in Aug 1914 and **9 in Sept 1914**. It fell to **1.4 by Dec 1914** after routine prophylaxis began around mid-October 1914. — "This was undoubtedly due to the introduction of prophylactic injections of antitetanic serum" — [Sir David Bruce, "Tetanus: Analysis of 1458 Cases…", *J Hygiene* 1920](https://core-prod.cambridgecore.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/060A7EEDA764B633DD29AE330F32CE95/S0022172400007671a.pdf/tetanus_analysis_of_1458_cases_which_occurred_in_home_military_hospitals_during_the_years_19141918.pdf) [PEER/primary]
23. The order: "every wounded man should receive an injection of 500 units of antitetanic serum." Protected cases had 22.5% mortality vs 53.3% unprotected; pre-serum tetanus mortality was about 85%. — same [PEER]
    - CONTRADICTION: a University of Toronto magazine piece says "about a third (32%)" of wounded British soldiers got tetanus in 1914 — [U of T Magazine](https://magazine.utoronto.ca/?p=34909). Bruce's official data (0.9% at peak) contradict this. **Do not use 32%.** Safest line: "in September 1914 about 9 in every 1,000 wounded got tetanus; within three months of routine antitoxin it was about 1.4."
24. Canada's antitoxin came from a backyard stable at 145 Barton Ave., Toronto, founded with $3,000 and five horses (later Connaught Laboratories). — "patriotic duty that we in Canada should manufacture tetanus antitoxin" — [U of T Magazine](https://magazine.utoronto.ca/?p=34909) [UNIV magazine]
25. **Dakin's solution:** 0.4–0.5% sodium hypochlorite buffered with 4% boric acid to pH 9–10. Chemist Henry Dakin (not a physician) and Nobel laureate Alexis Carrel (1912) used it at Carrel's hospital near Compiègne. It reportedly shortened healing by up to three weeks. — [Dakin's Solution, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/dakins-solution.html) [UNIV]
26. The New York Times headline on the method read "Remedy Said to Make Infection Impossible." — same page [UNIV]
27. The technique: excise damaged tissue, then continuously flush the wound with dilute hypochlorite through tubes. — [Ellis, *Br J Hosp Med* 2012 (Anniversaries)](https://storage.imrpress.com/IMR/hmed/application/10.12968/hmed.2012.73.10.594.pdf) [PEER]
    - The "1915" date for Carrel–Dakin was NOT confirmed on any page opened (PMC5252246 was blocked). Say "developed early in the war, by 1915–16," or verify before stating 1915.
28. No antibiotics: sulfonamides and antibiotics came later ("antibiotics arrived in the 1940s"). — [Dakin's Solution, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/dakins-solution.html). Caution: KUMC says sulfonamides were "late 1920s," which is off (sulfa drugs came in the 1930s). Penicillin 1928 was not verified this session.

## 2. Shock and blood

29. The 1917 British Medical Research Committee defined surgical shock as "a condition of circulatory failure due to deficient entry of blood into the heart." — [Scotland, *Stand To!*](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf) [SECONDARY quoting MRC]
30. Walter B. Cannon (Harvard, age 46) studied shock in wounded soldiers at Béthune. He injected sodium bicarbonate into men whose rapid breathing signalled acidosis, and the improvement was dramatic but temporary. He concluded "the primary condition was inadequate oxygen supply to the tissues." He published *Traumatic Shock* in 1923. — [MD magazine, Sept 1971, via Univ. of Pittsburgh HSLS](https://dec.hsls.pitt.edu/files/original/47a673bc3240caa378b48a5276ea24ff80a6aa84.pdf) [SECONDARY/archival]
31. Karl Landsteiner identified the ABO groups in 1900. — "Blood type C was later changed to O." — [AABB, Highlights of Transfusion Medicine History](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/resources/transfusion-medicine/highlights-of-transfusion-medicine-history) [medical society]. (The prompt said 1901, the publication year. Discovery 1900 / published 1901; safest on air: "around 1900.")
32. By the early 1910s, Ludvig Hektoen and W. L. Moss (Baltimore) urged ABO matching, and Ottenberg and Epstein promoted the "cross-match." — [Blood Transfusion in the First World War, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/blood-transfusion.html) [UNIV]
33. Sodium citrate as an anticoagulant was introduced independently in 1914–15 by Albert Hustin (Belgium), Luis Agote (Argentina) and Richard Lewisohn (New York). — same page [UNIV]. AABB dates Lewisohn (Mt. Sinai) to 1915; Weil (1915) showed refrigerated storage; Rous & Turner (1916) added citrate-glucose. — [AABB](https://www.aabb.org/news-resources/resources/transfusion-medicine/highlights-of-transfusion-medicine-history). UK National Archives credits Agote with the first anticoagulated transfusion in 1914 — [How three men saved countless lives](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war) [government archive blog]
34. Canadian Lawrence Bruce Robertson set up the first transfusion equipment at a Western Front casualty clearing station in spring 1917. His *Lancet* article of 24 Nov 1917 described 36 cases. — "an immediate and almost incredible improvement" — [UK National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war)
35. American Oswald Hope Robertson (Base Hospital No. 5, Boston) used group-O donors, citrated blood and Rous–Turner dextrose in glass bottles kept on ice. — [KUMC Blood Transfusion](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/blood-transfusion.html); stored up to **26 days** — [UK National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war)
36. Robertson's first blood bank: **22 units of universal-donor blood given to 20 wounded soldiers** "too injured to withstand surgery" (Stansbury & Hess, *Transfusion* 2017). — [Stanford Blood Center, 100th anniversary of the first blood bank](https://stanfordbloodcenter.org/100th-anniversary-first-blood-bank/) [medical institution citing PEER]
    - NLM says "22 successful transfusions with stored blood" — [NLM Charles Drew biography PDF](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/exhibition/education/charlesdrew/pdf/FatheroftheBloodBankBiography.pdf) [OFFICIAL]. The link to **Cambrai, Nov 1917** and survival numbers (e.g., "11 survived") were NOT found on any opened page. **On air:** "Robertson stored blood on ice and transfused about 20 badly wounded men" — avoid naming survivors unless verified.
    - DATE CONFLICT: AABB lists Robertson's depots under **1916**. Other sources say 1917, and he only arrived in France in 1917 (May 1917 per Invention & Technology). **Use 1917.**
37. Robertson's *BMJ*/*Lancet* paper (22 June 1918) said stored blood's effect was "fully as striking as that observed after the giving of freshly drawn blood," with blood pressure up 20–40 points. — [UK National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war). Also: "There is a definite need in front area medical work of a method for giving transfusions rapidly." — [Blood On Demand, Invention & Technology](https://www.inventionandtech.com/node/86455) [SECONDARY]. (The NatArchives says *Lancet*; Invention & Tech says *BMJ*. PMC indexes "Transfusion with Preserved Red Blood Cells" as BMJ 1918. Say "in a British medical journal in June 1918.")
38. Robertson received Britain's Distinguished Service Order. — [UK National Archives blog](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war)
39. A later series: 60 shocked patients transfused (Holmes à Court) and 11 died (18%). Field ambulance resuscitation teams did 52 transfusions in Aug–Sept 1918 (Butler). — [Scotland, *Stand To!*](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf) [SECONDARY citing MJA 1920 and official history]
40. In the AEF, shock treatment at triage included removing wet clothes, warming, hot drinks, morphine, IV saline and transfusion from matched donors. AEF consultants sent "one shock team" to Field Hospital No. 27 in July 1918. — [KUMC Triage](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html); AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]

## 3. Fractures: Thomas splint and X-ray

41. Hugh Owen Thomas (Liverpool bone-setter) described the splint in 1875 for TB joints and femur fractures. Robert Jones, his nephew, introduced it for acute femur fractures in WWI. — [RJAH NHS, The Thomas Splint](https://www.rjah.nhs.uk/media/qmjhtqh4/the-thomas-splint.pdf) [NHS hospital]
    - DATE CONFLICTS: SLQ says Thomas published in **1865** ([State Library of Queensland](https://www.slq.qld.gov.au/blog/thomas-splint-vastly-improving-mortality-rates-world-war-one)), while RJAH and JSOM say **1875**. Use 1875. For WWI adoption, SLQ says Jones recommended it in **Dec 1914** but it was "not routinely supplied until 1917." The UK National Archives says it was "Introduced in 1916 to the Front" ([A revolution in treatment](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/thomas-splint)), and JSOM says it was "officially distributed as the standard" in 1917. **On air:** "recommended from 1914, standard issue by 1917."
42. **The famous 80% → ~20% statistic, traced:**
    - The ~80% pre-splint mortality comes from Col. Sir Henry M. W. Gray, as cited in F. Watson, *Life of Sir Robert Jones* (1934). The post-splint figure is Gray's: at Arras (Apr–May 1917), **1,009 femur fractures, 15.6% mortality, 17.2% amputation** (Gray, *The Early Treatment of War Wounds*, 1919, pp. 59–60). — [Scotland, *Stand To!*](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf)
    - JSOM: "In 1916… Gray put femoral fracture mortality at roughly 80%… mortality fell to 15.6% of 1,009 cases"; another review of 3,141 patients gave 14%. — [J Special Operations Medicine, Summer 2014, p. 60](https://www.jsomonline.com/Flipbook/Journals/1402-Summer/files/basic-html/page60.html) [PEER]
    - The National Archives blog phrasing is "from 80% to 20%, in 1918" (footnoted but unnamed). Med-Chi says "1,006" cases and "from 4 in 5 to under 1 in 5." RJAH says "60 per cent reduction." Ellis (2015) says ~80% in 1914.
    - **Reliability:** the before figure is an early-war estimate from 1914–15 (Gray via Watson), not a controlled study. The after figure (15.6%) is a real case series of about 1,000. JSOM warns that ambulances and casualty clearing stations "cloud the effect." **Safest on air:** "Early in the war about 4 in 5 men with an open thigh-bone fracture died. By 1917, with the Thomas splint, British surgeon Henry Gray recorded about 1 in 6." Credit it as Gray's figures, not a precise trial.
43. Splint application had 12 stages, ideally done by a team of three. — [UK National Archives, A revolution in treatment (file AIR 2/136)](https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/thomas-splint)
44. Ellis: stretcher-bearers learned that "they could crawl out into no-man's land at night, splint the wounded soldier's leg." — [Ellis 2015](https://storage.imrpress.com/IMR/hmed/application/10.12968/hmed.2015.76.7.423.pdf) [PEER]
45. The British Army treated over 5,000 femoral fractures in the war's last 9 months. — [JSOM p. 60](https://www.jsomonline.com/Flipbook/Journals/1402-Summer/files/basic-html/page60.html)
46. AEF adoption: the Thomas splint was standard at AEF battalion aid stations ("Fracture immobilization with a Thomas Splint"). — [KUMC Regimental Aid Station](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/regimental-aid-station.html). (A dedicated source on AEF "splint teams" was not found.)
47. Marie Curie's "petites Curies": **20 X-ray vehicles** (the first ready by Oct 1914) and 200 fixed radiological units in the first two years. Her daughter Irène, 17, assisted. — [IEEE Spectrum, How Marie Curie helped save a million soldiers](https://spectrum.ieee.org/the-institute/ieee-history/how-marie-curie-helped-save-a-million-soldiers-during-world-war-i). The "a million soldiers" headline is not supported by a figure in the text. Avoid it, or say "by one popular estimate."
48. AEF X-ray: Base Hospital No. 28 took **3,601 X-rays in 1918** (2,012 of extremities) to locate shrapnel and fractures. There was no shielding. — "physicians who fluoroscoped patients with their bare hands, ultimately suffered radiation burns" — [Radiology, Base Hospital 28, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/base-hospital-28/clinical-services/radiology.html) [UNIV]
49. Field Hospital No. 12 at Soissons received X-ray equipment as augmentation. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]. Enlisted "manipulators" were trained to help surgeons take radiographs. — [Army Medical Dept. course MD0064](https://armymedical.tpub.com/MD0064/Early-Applications-Introduction-to-Radiography-12.htm) [OFFICIAL course text, mirror]

## 4. Surgery conditions, anesthesia, head and abdominal wounds

50. Anesthesia: chloroform and ether were standard issue. Geoffrey Marshall (British) found neither should be used on shocked patients, judged spinal anesthesia "disastrous," and advised nitrous oxide and oxygen. — "Operating and anaesthetising these soldiers might expedite death" — [Association of Anaesthetists, The Riddle of Shock](https://anaesthetists.org/Home/Heritage-centre/Learning/Previous-Exhibitions/The-Riddle-of-Shock-Anaesthesia-in-the-First-World-War) [medical society]
51. Syringes and needles were reused, sterilized and re-sharpened between patients. — same page
52. American teams in 1917 at British casualty clearing stations "operated twelve to sixteen hours a day and slept in tents." At Evacuation Hospital No. 4, teams worked 72 hours "with no rest." — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
53. At Soissons, about 1,500 patients lay awaiting surgery at Field Hospital No. 12, and surgeons worked 24-hour shifts for eight days. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
54. Evacuation Hospital No. 6 (Meuse-Argonne) admitted 1,100 in one day against a 900-bed nominal capacity, and operated on a record 350 cases in a day. Each team covered three operating tables. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
55. At Belleau Wood, Evacuation Hospital No. 7 had no operating equipment, so surgeons "performed all their operations on the four tables of Mobile Hospital No. 1." — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
56. Ambulances in the Meuse-Argonne covered 907,910 km and carried 132,065 sick and wounded. Hospital trains moved 151,045 patients on 408 trains (46 of them borrowed from the French). — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
57. Base Hospital No. 5 (Harvard, chief Harvey Cushing) took over British General Hospital No. 13 on 28 May 1917. On 4 Sept 1917 German night bombers hit it at Camiers. Lt. William T. Fitzsimmons and Privates Tugo, Rubino and Woods were killed: "the first A.E.F. deaths from enemy action." — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
58. **Cushing's head-wound numbers** (Casualty Clearing Station No. 46, Passchendaele, 22 July–1 Nov 1917): 219 head wounds, 133 brain wounds, and 55 deaths (41%). Mortality fell month by month: **24/44 (54.5%) → 18/44 (40.9%) → 13/45 (28.8%)**. — [Carey, "Cushing and the treatment of brain wounds during WWI," *J Neurosurg* 2011](https://d1xe7tfg0uwul9.cloudfront.net/cbc-portal/wp-content/uploads/2013/07/01062011-JNS.pdf) [PEER]
59. Cushing's techniques: gentle catheter "track suction" and a magnetized nail to pull out steel fragments (after de Martel). — "Aside, possibly, from the principle of track suction there is nothing original about [his] procedure" (Cushing). Carey argues Cushing adopted European methods without crediting them. — same [PEER]
60. Cushing's diaries became *From a Surgeon's Journal 1915–1918* (1936). He operated on Marne wounded in July 1918. — [Yale Library exhibit, Harvey Cushing](https://onlineexhibits.library.yale.edu/s/harvey-cushing/page/peter-bent-brigham-hospital) [UNIV]
61. Abdominal wounds: early surgery cut mortality from about 80% to 60% (Fraser & Drummond, *BMJ* 1917, 300 perforating wounds). — [Scotland, *Stand To!*](https://www.friendsofmillbank.org/downloads/Saving_Life_%26_Limb_Stand_To.pdf). Ellis (2015) gives "around 50–60%." **On air:** "more than half of men with belly wounds died even with surgery."

## 5. Gas

62. Chlorine at Ypres, 22 April 1915; phosgene first used Dec 1915; **mustard first used by Germany 12 July 1917 at Ypres**. — [US Army Heritage & Education Center, Gas Warfare in WWI](https://www.armyheritage.org/soldier-stories-information/gas-warfare-in-world-war-i/) [OFFICIAL]
63. **AEF gas totals:** 70,552 gas cases needing hospitalization and **1,221 deaths**, plus about 200 battlefield gas deaths. Gas was 27.3% of all American casualties and 31.4% of wounded. — [Task Force V Lessons Learned (DoD GulfLink, declassified)](https://gulflink.health.mil/declassdocs/af/19961205/120596_aaday_04.html) [OFFICIAL]. The USAHEC page gives "70,522… 31.49 percent… 1,221, mostly from mustard gas."
    - CONTRADICTION: 70,552 vs 70,522 is a likely transposition. The official source is *Medical Dept. vol. XIV, Medical Aspects of Gas Warfare* (1926), but its stats chapter was not reached in the NLM OCR. **On air:** "about 70,000 American gas casualties, about 1,200 of whom died in hospital." Low lethality but enormous numbers is the story.
64. First gassing of Americans: German phosgene/diphosgene shells hit the 1st Division on **2 Feb 1918** (ineffective). On the night of **26 Feb 1918**, gas injured or killed more than a third of the 225 men of the 1st Division in the area. — [University of Illinois Press blog (Thomas Faith, *Behind the Gas Mask*)](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/wordpress/the-word-discomfort-is-a-weak-description/) [UNIV press]. Science History Institute gives 5 Feb 1918 (Battery C, 7th Field Artillery). — [Distillations, A Strange and Formidable Weapon](https://sciencehistory.org/distillations/a-strange-and-formidable-weapon). Dates conflict. Safest: "in February 1918."
65. The US bought British small box respirators because American masks weren't ready. Straps caused headaches, saliva pooled at the mouthpiece and eyepieces fogged. Some commanders made men wear them as punishment. — "the word discomfort is a weak description" (Asst. Sec. of War Benedict Crowell) — [U. Illinois Press blog](https://www.press.uillinois.edu/wordpress/the-word-discomfort-is-a-weak-description/). The SBR was a two-piece design (canister plus rubber hose to the facepiece), and US troops often fitted US filters. — [Pritzker Military Museum](https://www.pritzkermilitary.org/node/695)
66. Mustard: symptoms are delayed by hours, and it penetrates clothing and leather and lingers for days or weeks. It causes blistering burns, especially of eyes and airways, and burns heal much more slowly than ordinary burns. — [NLM Circulating Now, "Gassed"](https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2017/10/04/gassed) [OFFICIAL]. Blisters lasted up to four weeks. — [USAHEC](https://www.armyheritage.org/soldier-stories-information/gas-warfare-in-world-war-i/)
67. In the first weeks of mustard (July 1917), over 14,000 gas cases reached British casualty clearing stations and about 500 died. — [NLM Circulating Now](https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2017/10/04/gassed). Mustard killed about 2.5 per 100 casualties, the lowest of any agent; most blinded men recovered. — [Jones, "Terror Weapons," *War in History* 2014 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5131841) [PEER]
68. Gas treatment at a US base hospital (BH 28, July 1918–Jan 1919): 930 gas patients out of 8,724. Gassed men were marked with a crayoned cross on the forehead. Skin was decontaminated by removing clothes, washing and hypochlorite soaks. Eyes were irrigated, with atropine for severe spasm. Lungs got bed rest and oxygen. Mobile degassing units gave a two-minute shower. — "Gas injuries had high morbidity, low mortality" — [Gas Injuries, Base Hospital 28, KUMC](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/base-hospital-28/clinical-services/gas-injuries.html) [UNIV]
69. On 26 Sept 1918 the First Army had four gas hospitals for the Meuse-Argonne. At Cantigny, Field Hospital No. 13 was the triage and gas hospital. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]
70. The AEF Gas Service was created 5 July 1917 (AEF GO 8; Lt. Col. Amos Fries). The **Chemical Warfare Service was created 28 June 1918** (War Dept. GO 62; Maj. Gen. William L. Sibert, first chief). — [US Army, Origins of the Chemical Corps (home.army.mil)](https://home.army.mil/wood/5217/8413/0800/Origins_Chem_Corps.pdf) [OFFICIAL]
71. The official US medical history opens its gas volume: "The extensive use of poisonous gases was one of the most important developments of the World War." — Med. Dept. vol. XIV (1926), p. 25, quoted at [NLM Circulating Now](https://circulatingnow.nlm.nih.gov/2017/10/04/gassed)

## 6. Trench foot and shell shock

72. Trench foot came from prolonged cold, wet feet in leaky boots. The British recorded over 20,000 cases in winter 1914/15 and 74,000 for the war (likely undercounted). Prevention was dry socks changed several times a day, **whale oil** rubbed in by soldiers massaging each other's feet, duckboards and foot inspections. — "in many units it was a chargeable offence to neglect the feet" — [Western Front Association, Trench Diseases of the First World War](https://www.westernfrontassociation.com/world-war-i-articles/trench-diseases-of-the-first-world-war/) [historical society]
73. Thomas W. Salmon visited Britain in May 1917 and proposed psychiatrists "as near the front as military exigency will permit." The three tiers were division level (rest, sedation, food), base hospital wards (up to 3 weeks), and **Base Hospital 117**, ~50 miles back, for up to 6 months. — [Pols & Oak, *Am J Public Health* 2007 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2089086) [PEER]. (ANC history page says BH 117 was "25 miles" from the front — [Army Nurse Corps Assn](https://e-anca.org/History/Topics-in-ANC-History/Psychiatric-Nursing-during-WWI). Distances conflict. Say "well behind the lines.")
74. The Army rejected the term "shell shock" in favor of "war neurosis." — [Psychiatric News, Thomas William Salmon](https://psychnews.psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/pn.37.13.0038). Inductee screening rejected about 2% on mental grounds. — [Pols & Oak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2089086)
75. Division psychiatrists sorted cases into shell fright, gas fright, hysteria, fatigue, malingering and cowardice. About 65% were kept at division and 35% sent on. — [KUMC Triage](https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/military-medical-operations/triage-field-hospital-section.html). At St. Mihiel, 282 shell-shock cases were seen and **225 returned to their units within three days**. — AMEDD-Ch4 [OFFICIAL]. In the Meuse-Argonne there were 2,029 neurological cases. — AMEDD-Ch4
76. CAUTION on success rates: one AEF front-line psychiatrist estimated up to 65% returned in 4–5 days ([Pols & Oak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2089086)). A 2007 study of a British forward unit found fewer than 20% returned directly to combat and called period statistics "both inaccurate and misleading." — [Jones, Thomas & Ironside, *Psychological Medicine* 2007](https://simonwessely.com/Downloads/Publications/Military/historical/Jones%202007%20-%20shell%20shock%20wwi%20pie.pdf) [PEER]. **On air:** "Army doctors reported most men went back within days; modern historians doubt those numbers."
77. In 1921, 27% of hospitalized ex-servicemen were classed as neuropsychiatric cases. — [Pols & Oak](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2089086) [PEER]
78. Nursing at BH 117: patients were woken at 4:00 a.m. for inspection. — "The social atmosphere of the ward was all important." (Salmon & Fenton, vol. X, 1929) — [ANCA](https://e-anca.org/History/Topics-in-ANC-History/Psychiatric-Nursing-during-WWI)
79. A total count of AEF neuropsychiatric cases was NOT found on any opened page (vol. X stats were paywalled or blocked). Do not state a total.

## Best story moments (chapter hooks)
1. **The packet in his pocket**: every doughboy carried two bandages and two safety pins. At the aid station 500–1,000 yards back he got iodine, 500 units of tetanus serum and a quarter-grain of morphine (KUMC). Tetanus had hit 9 per 1,000 wounded in Sept 1914 and fell to 1.4 once every wounded man got the shot (Bruce 1920).
2. **The 12-hour clock**: manured French farmland seeded wounds with *Clostridia*. Without surgery within about 12 hours gas gangrene threatened. A US base-hospital lab built its anaerobic incubator out of a gasoline can.
3. **Blood on ice**: Oswald Robertson filled glass bottles with group-O blood, kept them on ice for up to 26 days, and gave 22 units to 20 men too broken for surgery. Of stored blood he wrote that the effect was "fully as striking" as fresh.
4. **4 in 5 → 1 in 6**: early in the war a shattered thigh bone usually killed. At Arras in 1917, Henry Gray splinted 1,009 such men and 15.6% died. Stretcher-bearers crawled into no-man's-land at night to strap the Thomas splint on.
5. **Cushing's learning curve**: in a tent hospital at Passchendaele, Harvey Cushing pulled steel from brains with a magnetized nail. His brain-wound deaths fell from 54.5% to 28.8% in three months.
6. **The first Americans killed by the enemy were medics**: 4 Sept 1917, German bombers hit Base Hospital No. 5 at Camiers despite its Red Cross markings.
7. **The crayon cross**: gassed men arrived with a cross crayoned on their foreheads and got a two-minute shower, eye irrigation and oxygen. About 70,000 American gas cases, about 1,200 hospital deaths, and blisters that lasted weeks.
8. **Close to the front**: Salmon's rule put psychiatrists "as near the front as military exigency will permit." At St. Mihiel 225 of 282 shell-shocked men went back within three days, though historians now question how many truly returned to combat.
