## 1. Antietam, September 17, 1862

1. The NPS calls Antietam the bloodiest single day in American military history. — "began the single bloodiest day in American military history" — [NPS, Lincoln Home NHS: Antietam](https://home.nps.gov/liho/learn/historyculture/antietam.htm). The Antietam park's own homepage is titled "The Bloodiest Day in American History" — [NPS Antietam](https://www.nps.gov/anti/index.htm)
2. NPS round figure for the day: about 23,000 casualties in twelve hours. — "23,000 soldiers were killed, wounded or missing after twelve hours of savage combat on September 17, 1862." — [NPS Antietam homepage](https://www.nps.gov/anti/index.htm)
3. American Battlefield Trust breakdown: Union 12,401 (2,108 killed, 9,540 wounded, 753 missing/captured); Confederate 10,316 (1,546 killed, 7,752 wounded, 1,018 missing/captured); total 22,717 **[ESTIMATE]**. — "Antietam, the deadliest one-day battle in American military history" — [ABT: Antietam](https://www.battlefields.org/antietam)
4. NPS Civil War facts page gives 22,726 casualties at Antietam and the single-day superlative. — "had the greatest number of casualties of any single-day battle" — [NPS Civil War Facts](https://nps.gov/subjects/civilwar/facts.htm)
   - **[CONFLICT, minor]** 23,000 (NPS park, rounded) vs 22,717 (ABT) vs 22,726 (NPS facts) vs 22,720 (NPS casualties page). **SAFE ON AIR:** "about 23,000 men killed, wounded or missing in a single day."
5. NPS location estimates: the Cornfield alone cost about 8,200 casualties (both sides); Bloody Lane about 5,400 **[ESTIMATE]**. — "These are estimates" — [NPS Antietam Casualties](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/casualties.htm)
6. Letterman's own count of Union wounded at Antietam: 8,350, and he says it was an undercount. — "they gave in this battle a total of eight thousand three hundred and fifty, but there were many cases of slight wounds not recorded." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 44, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/medicalrecollect00lettuoft)
7. On top of his own men, Letterman's department took in more than 2,000 Confederate wounded left behind. — "superintending these prisoners, who numbered over two thousand" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 46, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/medicalrecollect00lettuoft)
8. Letterman's own wording on clearing the field is "before two o'clock the day following the battle" (for the right wing), not a flat "24 hours." — "the wounded were brought from our right before two o'clock the day following the battle." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 42, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/medicalrecollect00lettuoft)
9. He admits wounded lay between the lines until the enemy pulled back, and some were simply missed. — "some men, having been overlooked, remained on the field after the others had been removed. But these instances were rare" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 43
10. Yet he calls the evacuation careful and quick. — "the wounded were removed from that sanguinary field in so careful and expeditious a manner." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 44
11. Modern secondary sources turn this into "all wounded removed within 24 hours." — "were able to remove all of the wounded from the field in just 24 hours" — [ABT: Jonathan Letterman biography](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/biographies/jonathan-letterman); same claim at [NMCWM: Major Jonathan Letterman](https://www.civilwarmed.org/jonathanletterman/) ("removed all wounded from the battlefield within 24 hours")
12. The Army's official history is more careful, and records criticism. — "some of the more than 8,000 wounded remained on the battlefield as much as twenty-four hours." — GILLETT, p. 193, [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/TheArmyMedicalDepartment18181865)
13. Critics at the time attacked the Antietam ambulance service. — "gross mismanagement and inefficiency, ... lack of system and control" — critics quoted in GILLETT, p. 192
    - **[CONFLICT]** "All wounded off the field within 24 hours" (ABT, NMCWM) vs Letterman's "before two o'clock the day following" for the right wing, with stragglers missed, and Gillett's "as much as twenty-four hours" with contemporary criticism. **SAFE ON AIR:** "Letterman reported that his wounded on the right of the line were brought in by the next afternoon. Most of the field was cleared in about a day, though some men were missed and lay out longer."
14. Letterman says the ambulance system wasn't fully working at Antietam. Ambulances had been left behind or lost, and 200 new ones were "unorganized." — "although two hundred had been distributed just before the action, they were unorganized." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 42
15. Hospitals were placed by division, and Letterman preferred barns with hay to houses. — "to choose barns well provided with hay and straw, as preferable to houses, since they were better ventilated" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 39
16. Wounded were also laid in the open air beside the barns. — "to place the wounded in the open air near the barns, rather than in badly-constructed houses" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 39
17. Even so, the field hospitals were not always out of artillery range. — "the hospitals were not always beyond the reach of the enemy's guns." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 39
18. The line ran 6–7 miles, so there were "very numerous" hospitals. I found no exact count from a source I could open. — "The line of battle was between six and seven miles in length, the hospitals were therefore very numerous" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 41
19. Letterman's observation: men in the open air did better than men in barns, and men in barns did better than men in houses. — "Those in houses progressed less favorably than those in barns, those in the latter buildings less favorably than those in the open air" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 48
20. Pry House: McClellan's headquarters, and later a hospital (for Gen. Israel Richardson, among others). — "The house was used as a hospital for Richardson and possibly other officers for about two months." — [NPS: The Pry Farm](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/the-pry-farm.htm)
21. Pry Barn was a Union field hospital. — "the Pry Barn was used as a field hospital for Union soldiers until they could be moved to Frederick, MD." — [NPS: The Pry Farm](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/the-pry-farm.htm)
22. Hooker's wound was treated at the Pry House. — "his wounded foot that had filled his boot with blood" — [NPS: The Pry Farm](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/the-pry-farm.htm)
23. Today the Pry House is a field hospital museum run by the National Museum of Civil War Medicine. — "The museum is operated by the National Museum of Civil War Medicine." — [NPS: Pry House Field Hospital Museum](https://www.nps.gov/anti/planyourvisit/pryhouse.htm?ms=home)
24. Roulette Farm barn was a field hospital. — "The barn was used as a field hospital for those wounded in the action." — [NPS: The Roulette Farm](https://www.nps.gov/places/the-roulette-farm.htm)
25. After the battle Letterman set up two large tent "camp hospitals," the first of their kind in the US. The larger was the Antietam Hospital at Smoketown, with about 600 beds. — "These institutions were the first of the kind attempted in this country" — LETTERMAN 1866, pp. 46–47
26. The most serious cases were carried to these camps by hand rather than by wagon. — "to these all the most serious cases were carried by hand." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 47
27. The route home ran by ambulance to Frederick (with a rest stop at Middletown), then by train to Baltimore and Washington. By Oct. 30, Frederick's hospitals held more than 5,000 patients. — "On the 30th of October the hospitals of this city contained over five thousand patients" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 45
28. Relatives arrived to take badly wounded men home, and Letterman thought it often killed them. — "It was impossible to convince them that the removal of a dangerously wounded man would be made at the risk of his life" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 47
29. Surgeons died at Antietam too, including Asst. Surgeon Revere of the 20th Massachusetts, killed "by the hands of the enemy." — "Some Medical officers lost their lives in their devotion to duty on the battle-field of Antietam" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 49

## 2. The Minié ball

30. Description: the rifle-musket bullet was elongated, with an expanding base (plain or hollow) that gripped the rifling and made it spin. — "elongated bullets usually have an expansive base, either plain or hollowed, which by the explosion of the charge is intended to fill the grooves" — MSHWR, Ch. XII (Projectiles), [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov)
31. Official specification: the Springfield rifled-musket ball was .58 caliber and weighed 500 grains (just over an ounce). — "Springfield rifled musket ball, calibre .58, weight 500 grains" — MSHWR, Fig. 373 caption
32. A National Library of Medicine exhibit uses a 510-grain Minié ball from a .58 Springfield in a modern bone study. — "Human distal femur shot with a 510-grain lead Minié ball fired from a .58 caliber Springfield Model 1862 rifle." — [NLM Visible Proofs](https://www.nlm.nih.gov/visibleproofs/galleries/exhibition/body_image_25.html)
33. It was soft lead with rings at the base, invented by French officers in 1849. — "The bullet was made out of soft lead, had a conical shape, and had anywhere from two to four rings at the base." — [Gettysburg Compiler (Gettysburg College), "Small but Deadly: The Minié Ball"](https://gettysburgcompiler.org/2019/04/30/small-but-deadly-the-minie-ball/) (student-written; use for color, not as sole source)
34. Why it did so much damage: the soft lead flattened on impact. — "the soft lead of the minie ball caused the ball to flatten out upon hitting its target" — [Gettysburg Compiler](https://gettysburgcompiler.org/2019/04/30/small-but-deadly-the-minie-ball/)
35. The Army's National Museum of Health and Medicine summarizes the effect. — "shattered bone and shredded flesh became the calling cards of the minie ball." — [NMHM: To Bind Up the Nation's Wounds](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_02)
36. The 1880s official history noted that bullets deformed in every conceivable way. — "[T]he distortions of bullets were of all imaginable forms." — MSHWR as quoted in [Civil War Monitor, "When Bullets Hit Bodies"](https://www.civilwarmonitor.com/article/when-bullets-hit-bodies/)
37. Amputation was often the practical answer. — "amputation made a complex wound simple." — [NMHM exhibit](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_02)
    - Non-graphic narration idea: a soft, heavy, slow lead slug flattened and broke bone into splinters instead of drilling a neat hole. Splintered long bones were very hard to save with 1860s tools. (This paraphrases sources 34–37. Keep it there.)
38. Scale of the official record: 246,712 wounds by weapons were treated, 245,790 of them gunshot. Only 922 (0.37%) were sabre or bayonet wounds. — "Of these, two hundred and forty-five thousand seven hundred and ninety (245,790) were shot wounds" — MSHWR, Ch. XII, p. ~687
39. **Extremities ~71%:** 35.71% of treated wounds were to the arms and 35.15% to the legs. — "10.77 per cent, were injuries of the head, face, and neck, 18.37 of the trunk, 35.71 of the upper extremities, and 35.15 of the lower extremities." — MSHWR, Ch. XII, p. ~691
40. The same volume gives the combined total of 70.86% and its caveat: the killed are not counted. — "the injuries of the extremities form 70.86 per cent, of the total number of wounded" — MSHWR, Ch. XII, p. ~692
    - **SAFE ON AIR:** "Of the wounds Union surgeons recorded treating, about seven in ten were in the arms or legs. Men hit in the head or body more often never reached a surgeon at all." (The second sentence is MSHWR's own caveat: the killed are not included.)
41. Civil War Monitor estimate: the minié caused about 76% of battlefield injuries **[ESTIMATE]**. — "inflicted an estimated 76% of the war's battlefield injuries" — [Civil War Monitor](https://www.civilwarmonitor.com/article/when-bullets-hit-bodies/)

## 3. Before Letterman: Bull Run, the Peninsula, Second Manassas

42. First Bull Run (1861): wounded were left on the field and walking wounded dragged themselves to Washington. — "some of whom had been left for long periods unattended on the field" — GILLETT, p. 166
43. First Bull Run: hired civilian drivers abandoned the wounded. — "the civilian drivers abandoned their posts" — ATKINSON, [npshistory.com PDF](https://www.npshistory.com/series/symposia/gettysburg_seminars/13/essay5.pdf)
44. On the Peninsula, 1862, there were few ambulances. Many were two-wheelers that Medical Director Tripler called worthless. — "many of these were two-wheelers, which Tripler regarded as 'good for nothing.'" — GILLETT, p. ~188
45. June 28, 1862: with too few ambulances, Tripler had to leave more than 600 wounded to the enemy at White House. — "Tripler had to order more than 600 wounded abandoned to the enemy because he had so few ambulances" — GILLETT, p. 189
46. Before Letterman, nobody owned the ambulances, and officers used them for their own convenience. — "They were under the control both of Medical officers and Quartermasters, and, as a natural consequence, little care was exercised over them by either." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 22
47. — "too often used as if they had been made for the convenience of commanding officers." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 23
48. Second Manassas (Aug. 28–30, 1862): drivers were untrained and the wounded lay out for a day or more. — "Those unable to walk lay by the thousands on the ground for twenty-four and more hours unattended." — GILLETT, p. 191
49. Second Manassas: more than 8,000 Union casualties overwhelmed the system. — "the entire system was not prepared for the more than 8,000 casualties that flooded in upon it." — GILLETT, p. 192
50. About ten days later, around 600 wounded were still waiting to be taken off the field. — "Ten days after the battle, 600 wounded still needed evacuation" — ATKINSON, [npshistory.com PDF](https://www.npshistory.com/series/symposia/gettysburg_seminars/13/essay5.pdf)
51. Drunk and frightened drivers: 250 ambulances were scraped together in Washington in one afternoon. A Quartermaster Department reply (Official Records) concedes some drivers ran or got drunk. — "a few of the drivers got scared and ran away, a few got drunk" — Official Records, Series III, Vol. II, p. 699, [civilwar.com transcription](https://civilwar.com/official-record/990-union-letters-orders-reports/270318-699-series-iii-volume-ii-serial-123-union-letters-orders-reports.html)
52. The same document: the 250 ambulances were mostly new, and the horses had never been harnessed. — "By 6 p. m. of that day 250 ambulances were collected and fitted out, most of them new and harnessed for the first time." — OR Ser. III, Vol. II, p. 699 (same link)
    - **SAFE ON AIR:** "Hastily hired drivers, some of whom, by the Army's own admission, ran off or got drunk."
53. Clara Barton reached the Second Manassas field and described what she saw. — "ghastly heaps of cut off arms and legs" — Barton as quoted in ATKINSON
54. Letterman heard that the few Army of the Potomac ambulances working under his new system at Pope's battles outperformed the rest. — "was very striking in favor of the former." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 31
55. Surgeon General William A. Hammond, a reformer who was in office from spring 1862, chose Letterman to replace Tripler. — "Hammond decided to replace Tripler with Jonathan Letterman." — ATKINSON
56. Hammond pushed for an Army-wide ambulance corps, but Secretary of War Stanton rejected the Army-wide plan (Aug. 29, 1862). — "increase the expenses and immobility of our army ... without any corresponding advantages" — Stanton quoted in GILLETT, p. 191
57. General-in-Chief Halleck agreed with Stanton weeks later, so the system stayed limited to the Army of the Potomac until 1864. — "an opinion Maj. Gen. Henry W. Halleck echoed less than three weeks later." — GILLETT, p. 191
58. The 1864 law was credited to Hammond's groundwork plus Letterman's model. — "the crop that Hammond, in this instance with Letterman's aid, had sown." — GILLETT, p. 227

## 4. Jonathan Letterman and his system

59. Dates in his own words: orders dated June 23, 1862; reported July 1; took charge July 4, 1862. — "I reported on the 1st day of July to Major-General McClellan at Haxhall's Landing" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 5
60. — "and on the 4th took charge of the Medical Department of that army." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 5
    - **[CONFLICT]** NPS and ABT say he was "appointed" or "named" medical director in **June 1862** ([NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/jonathan-letterman.htm), [ABT](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/biographies/jonathan-letterman)). NMCWM and JBSA say **July 1862**. ABT also says "medical director of the entire army," which is wrong: it was the Army of the Potomac. **SAFE ON AIR:** "In the summer of 1862 — ordered in June, in charge by July 4th — Jonathan Letterman became medical director of the Army of the Potomac."
61. **The order is SPECIAL Orders No. 147, not General Orders.** It was issued at Harrison's Landing, Va., on Aug. 2, 1862, over McClellan's name. — "Camp near Harrison's Landing, Va., Aug. 2, 1862. SPECIAL ORDERS, No. 147." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 24 (full text of the order is printed there)
    - Also given as "Special Order 147" in a peer-reviewed review on [PMC (PMC1863579)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1863579). **SAFE ON AIR:** "Special Orders Number 147, August 2nd, 1862."
62. Command structure: a captain per army corps, a first lieutenant per division, a second lieutenant per brigade, a sergeant per regiment. — "a captain to each Army Corps, as the commandant of the ambulance corps ; a first lieutenant for a division, second lieutenant for a brigade, and a sergeant for each regiment." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 24
63. Vehicles per regiment: one transport cart, one four-horse and two two-horse ambulances, with two stretchers per ambulance. — "one transport cart, one four-horse and two two-horse ambulances for a regiment" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 24
64. Dedicated crew: two men and a driver per ambulance. — "two men and a driver to each ambulance, and one driver to each transport cart." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 24
65. Light two-horse ambulances took men off the field. Four-horse ambulances moved treated men further back. — "using the light two-horse ambulances for bringing men from the field, and the four-horse ones for carrying those already attended to further to the rear" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 26
66. The crews drilled at stretcher-bearing, down to which foot to step off with. — "the front man steps off with the left foot and the rear man with the right" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 25
67. Water kegs were refilled daily so the ambulances could move at a moment's notice. — "the kegs rinsed and filled daily with fresh water, that he may be able to move at any moment." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 25
68. The ambulance men wore a green uniform badge. — "for privates, a green band, two inches broad, around the cap, a green half-chevron, two inches broad, on each arm above the elbow" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 29
69. Only the ambulance corps could carry wounded from the field, which ended the practice of healthy comrades leaving the line to "help." — "No person will be allowed to carry from the field any wounded or sick except this corps." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 30
70. The crews were permanently detailed soldiers, not teamsters or bandsmen. — "They were soldiers permanently detailed from the ranks for ambulance work." — GILLETT, p. 191
71. **Supply reform (Oct. 1862):** medical supplies were issued by brigade, so a regiment's medicines weren't dumped whenever wagons were needed. — "supplies would be issued by brigade and doled out to the individual regiments as needed." — GILLETT, p. 193
72. Letterman's reason: officers treated medical supplies as the least important cargo. — "It would appear that many officers consider medical supplies to be the least important in an army" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 32
73. **Field-hospital circular, Oct. 30, 1862:** a hospital per division, set up before the battle. — "Previous to an engagement there will be established in each Corps an hospital for each division" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 58
74. Three operating surgeons per division hospital, chosen for skill, not rank. — "These officers will be selected from the division without regard to rank, but solely on account of their known prudence, judgment, and skill." — LETTERMAN 1866, pp. 60–61
75. Doubtful cases were decided by majority vote of the three. — "In all doubtful cases they will consult together, and a majority of them shall decide upon the expediency and character of the operation." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 60
76. One assistant to give the anaesthetic. (Hand-off to Part B.) — "It is suggested that one of these assistants be selected to administer the anaesthetic." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 61
77. The goal was to operate early and avoid post-battle chaos. — "it is hoped that the confusion and the delay in performing the necessary operations so often existing after a battle, will be avoided" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 61
78. Records were kept for every case, and every grave was marked. — "the grave is marked with a head-board, with the name, rank, company, and regiment legibly inscribed upon it." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 60
79. **Tier 1, the regimental aid station:** one medical officer per regiment set up just behind the line for immediate aid. — "establish themselves, each one at a temporary depot, at such a distance or situation in the rear of his regiment as will insure safety to the wounded" — LETTERMAN 1866, pp. 61–62
80. The chain of care as practised at Fredericksburg (Dec. 1862): stretcher-bearers to the brigade aid point, then by ambulance to the division hospital. — "Here the wounded would receive such attendance as was imperatively required, after which they would be conveyed to their division hospitals by the ambulances." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 71
81. NPS summary of the three tiers. — "three clear stations for wounded soldiers to be moved through: field dressing stations, field hospitals, and permanent hospitals." — [NPS: Jonathan Letterman](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/jonathan-letterman.htm)
82. Fredericksburg, night of Dec. 13, 1862: ambulance men searched the field in the dark without lanterns because a light drew sniper fire. — "the glimmer of a candle invariably called forth a shot from a sharpshooting picket. They were obliged to grope their way" — LETTERMAN 1866, pp. 72–73
83. By dawn the field was cleared of all wounded within Union lines. — "before dawn all the wounded, who were not beyond our lines, were taken to the hospitals prepared for them in the rear." — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 73
84. Title: NPS and ABT use "Father of Modern Battlefield Medicine." Health.mil and JBSA use "Father of Battlefield Medicine." — "Father of Modern Battlefield Medicine" — [NPS](https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/jonathan-letterman.htm); "Father of Battlefield Medicine" — [JBSA, Jan. 3, 2024](https://www.jbsa.mil/News/News/Article/3630730/jbsa-fort-sam-houston-street-renamed-after-civil-war-surgeon-soldier/)
    - **SAFE ON AIR:** "Today he's often called the father of modern battlefield medicine."
85. **Ambulance Corps Act**, approved March 11, 1864. — "An Act to establish a uniform System of Ambulances in the Armies of the United States." ... "Approved, March 11, 1864." — [13 Stat. 20, govinfo.gov](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-13/pdf/STATUTE-13-Pg20.pdf)
86. The Act kept ambulances strictly for the wounded. — "the ambulances in the armies of the United States shall be used only for the transportation of the sick and wounded" — [13 Stat. 20, §8](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/STATUTE-13/pdf/STATUTE-13-Pg20.pdf)
87. The Act scaled ambulances to regiment size: an infantry regiment of 500+ men got three. — "An infantry regiment of 500 or more men would have three ambulances, a regiment of fewer than 200 only one." — GILLETT, p. 228
88. The Army's verdict on its authorship. — "The basic plan for the ambulance corps was unmistakably Letterman's." — GILLETT, p. 228
89. Letterman resigned in December 1864, died March 15, 1872, and is buried at Arlington. His gravestone praises the man "who brought order and efficiency in to the Medical Service." — [ABT biography](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/biographies/jonathan-letterman)

## 5. Ambulances and stretcher bearers

90. **Two-wheeled ambulances, in the government's own words:** — "their motion was intolerable and excruciating; wounded men begged to be taken out, wounded officers insisted upon leaving them" — MSHWR, Ch. XV (Ambulance Wagons), p. ~948
91. The Army ordered 200 two-wheelers built in May 1861, so these were the ambulances of the early war. — "two hundred of the two-wheeled ambulances be immediately constructed by the Quartermaster's Department." — Acting SG R.C. Wood, May 20, 1861, quoted in MSHWR p. ~948
92. Four-wheelers replaced them. The first was the Tripler wagon (four horses, ten men: four lying, six seated). — "It was constructed to carry ten men, four lying at length and six seated, and required four horses." — MSHWR p. ~948
93. The Rucker wagon was the best of the late war. — "The most serviceable ambulance wagon used during the latter part of the war was that designed by Brigadier General D. H. Rucker" — MSHWR p. ~952
94. The Rucker weighed about 1,120 lb, sat on platform springs, carried a water cask under the driver's seat, and its stretchers folded into seats. — "The weight was about one thousand one hundred and twenty pounds, exceeding that of the Wheeling" — MSHWR p. ~953
95. The Rosecrans/"Wheeling" ambulance was the most widely used model (about 750 lb, two horses, springs). — "Suspension springs helped reduced the jarring on injured passengers" — [ABT / Dana Shoaf, "The Rosecrans Ambulance," 2024](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/the-rosecrans-ambulance) (sic: "reduced")
96. Stretchers weighed 23–26 lb before a man was put on them. — "averaging between 23 and 26 pounds" — [NMCWM: Stretchers Used by the Union Army](https://www.civilwarmed.org/stretchers/)
97. Before the corps, stretcher duty often fell to young musicians. — "young lads physically incapable of such fatiguing duty" — Dr. R. S. Vickery, quoted at [NMCWM: Stretchers](https://www.civilwarmed.org/stretchers/)
98. Ambulance work was dangerous. At Gettysburg Letterman lists an ambulance lieutenant and two privates killed, 17 privates wounded, and 8 ambulances damaged. — "Lieutenant H. E. Clark, Fifth New York Volunteers, and two privates were killed, seventeen privates wounded" — LETTERMAN 1866, p. 158

## 6. Gettysburg, July 1–3, 1863

99. **Letterman's own total: 20,995 wounded** for the Medical Department to care for (14,193 Union and 6,802 Confederate). — "making twenty thousand nine hundred and ninety-five to be provided for by the Medical Department." — LETTERMAN 1866, pp. 159–160
100. ABT rounding. — "About 14,000 wounded Union and 6,000 wounded Confederate soldiers were left in Gettysburg" — [ABT: Camp Letterman, Gettysburg (Paige Gibbons Backus)](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/camp-letterman-gettysburg)
    - **[CONFLICT, minor]** About 21,000 (Letterman: 20,995) vs about 20,000 (ABT) vs "22,000" (PMC review). **SAFE ON AIR:** "Some 21,000 wounded men, Union and Confederate, were left in and around a town of about 2,400 people." (The 2,400 town population is NOT verified here; drop it unless Part B confirms it.)

## 1. Anesthesia

1. Anesthetics were used in at least 80,000 Union cases. The army's own history calls this a minimum figure. — "as near as can be ascertained they were used in no less than eighty thousand (80,000) instances." — [MSH-Surg3, Anaesthetics chapter](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
2. The record shows only 254 cases where surgeons said no anesthetic was given. — "it is possible that in two hundred and fifty-four cases in which it was asserted that no anaesthetic was given" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
3. The American Battlefield Trust repeats the same figure, rounded. — "with fewer than 300 done without the use of anesthesia" — [Backus, "A Fight for Life or Death," battlefields.org](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/fight-life-or-death)
4. HistoryNet (Kyle Dalton of the National Museum of Civil War Medicine) works this out as a 99.7% anesthesia rate. — "only 254 without (a rate of 99.7 percent)." — [HistoryNet, "Civil War Anesthesia Wasn't Just a Slug of Whiskey"](https://historynet.com/how-civil-war-battlefield-surgery-made-anesthesia-mainstream/)
5. Chloroform was the main agent. In the 8,900 major operations where the agent is known, chloroform was used in 76.2%, ether in 14.7% and a mixture in 9.1%. — "chloroform was used in six thousand seven hundred and eighty-four, or 76.2 per cent., ether in one thousand three hundred and five, or 14.7 per cent." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
6. Field hospitals used chloroform and general hospitals often used ether. — "the operations performed in the field hospitals were examined, where chloroform was almost uniformly used" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
7. The 1883 MSH gives the reasons field surgeons preferred chloroform: it worked fast and only a small quantity was needed. — "From the rapidity of its effects, and from the small quantity required … chloroform was preferred by nearly all the field surgeons" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
8. A modern historian adds that chloroform does not burn, which mattered near candles and lanterns. — "Chloroform was preferred because it had a quicker onset of action, could be used in small volumes, and was nonflammable." — Robert Reilly, quoted in [HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/how-civil-war-battlefield-surgery-made-anesthesia-mainstream/). The "nonflammable" point comes from this modern source, not from the MSH quote above.
9. **Deaths from anesthesia: 43 in total (37 chloroform, 4 ether, 2 mixed) among the 8,900 cases where the agent is known.** Chloroform: "death was ascribed to this agent in thirty-seven, or 5.4 per thousand". Ether: "four deaths, or 3.0 per thousand". Mixture: "two deaths, or 2.4 per thousand". — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt) **[DISAGREE]** The PBS (Devine) and *Military Medicine* (Cutter & Clarke 2014) articles quote "37 deaths (5.4 per thousand)" next to the 80,000 figure. That pairing is a misreading: 5.4 per thousand is the rate among the 6,784 known chloroform cases, not among 80,000. **SAFE ON AIR:** "Among nearly nine thousand recorded operations where the drug is known, forty-three deaths were blamed on the anesthetic itself."
10. The cone method, as described by a wartime surgeon's manual. — "The best apparatus is a folded cloth in the form of a cone, in the apex of which a small piece of sponge is placed." — J. Julian Chisolm (Confederate manual, 1861), quoted in [HistoryNet](https://historynet.com/how-civil-war-battlefield-surgery-made-anesthesia-mainstream/)
11. The cone method, as described in a modern account. — "place the chloroform on a piece of cotton or towel, which had been fashioned into a cone" — [Devine, PBS Mercy Street Revealed](https://www.pbs.org/blogs/mercy-street-revealed/chloroform-and-the-american-civil-war-the-art-of-practice-and-the-science-of-medicine/index.html)
12. Onset time, according to a peer-reviewed source. — "On average, it took about 9 minutes for the chloroform to take effect." — [Cutter & Clarke, "Anesthesia Advances During the Civil War," *Military Medicine* 179(12), 2014 (DTIC PDF)](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1066827.pdf)
13. The "bite the bullet" myth is rejected in the peer-reviewed literature. — "This information should put to rest the stories of soldiers biting on bullets" — [Cutter & Clarke, *Military Medicine* 2014](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1066827.pdf). **SAFE ON AIR:** "The image of a soldier biting a bullet while the saw went in is mostly myth. In the Union army almost every recorded operation used chloroform or ether."
14. Supplies sometimes ran short. A Confederate surgeon wrote about rationing chloroform. — "we had none to waste." — Surgeon F. E. Daniel, quoted by the [National Museum of Civil War Medicine, "The Exception Not the Rule," Part 2](https://www.civilwarmed.org/unanesthetized-surgery2/)
15. Surgeons waited until the patient was fully under. — "no attempt was made to cut till the patient was limber." — F. E. Daniel, quoted by [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/unanesthetized-surgery2/)
16. Operations without anesthesia did happen, but rarely. The museum has tracked down only about ten capital amputations done without it. — "about ten that we've tracked down" — [NMCWM, Part 2](https://www.civilwarmed.org/unanesthetized-surgery2/) **[ESTIMATE]** This is the museum's own count.
17. Across both armies, general anesthesia may have been used about 125,000 times. — "Perhaps 125,000 surgeries involving general anesthesia were performed on both sides during the war" — [HistoryNet (Dalton)](https://historynet.com/how-civil-war-battlefield-surgery-made-anesthesia-mainstream/) **[ESTIMATE]** The estimate is Dalton's.

## 2. Amputation

18. **Total recorded Union amputations: 29,980.** — "making the total number of amputations twenty-nine thousand nine hundred and eighty (29,980)." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
19. The MSH itself says the true number was higher. Early-war reports were thin, and some officers were treated privately. — "the whole number of amputations performed for injuries received during the war would undoubtedly exceed that number." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
20. Of 174,206 Union shot wounds of the limbs, 29,426 were treated by amputation. The 29,980 total also counts 305 amputations after excision and 249 re-amputations. — "The shot wounds of the extremities number one hundred and seventy-four thousand two hundred and six (174,206)" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
21. **Overall amputation mortality was 26.3%.** Table CLXVIII gives 29,980 cases: 20,802 recovered, 7,459 died and 1,719 had unknown outcomes. — "Of the approximately 30,000 amputations performed in the Civil War there was a 26.3-percent mortality rate." — [Stanley B. Burns MD, PBS Mercy Street](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/surgery-civil-war). The primary table is in [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt).
22. **Mortality by site (MSH-Surg3, 1883).** Hip joint 83.3%, knee 57.5%, thigh 54.2%, leg 33.2%, shoulder 29.1%, ankle 25.1%, upper arm 23.8%, forearm 14.0%, wrist 10.4%, elbow 7.6%, foot 5.7%, hand and fingers 2.9%. — "the operations at the hip gave the largest ratio of mortality, 83.3 per cent.; next come the amputations through the knee joint with 57.5 per cent." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
23. The general rule: the closer to the trunk, the deadlier the amputation. — "the ratio of mortality … decreases with the increasing distance of the point of ablation from the trunk." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt). **SAFE ON AIR:** "Lose a finger and you almost certainly lived. Lose a leg at the thigh and it was a coin toss. At the hip, more than four in five died."
24. Hip-joint amputations were rare and usually fatal: 66 cases and 55 deaths. — "Of 66 cases of amputation of the hip-joint, 55, or 83.3 per cent died." — W. W. Keen, quoted in [Henretta/Macmillan, "Thinking Like a Historian"](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/henretta8e/henretta8e_ch14_10.html). This agrees with [Cutter, *Military Medicine* 2015](https://apps.dtic.mil/sti/pdfs/AD1066825.pdf).
25. **Primary amputations (within 48 hours) had 23.9% mortality.** — "Sixteen thousand two hundred and thirty-eight (16,238) amputations belong in the group of primary operations … 3,992, or 23.9 per cent., terminated in death" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
26. **Intermediary amputations (day 3 to day 30, during the inflammation phase) had 34.8% mortality, the worst group.** Secondary amputations (after day 30) had 28.8%. — "The primary operations gave the best chance for life. The operations performed during the intermediary or inflammatory period were the most unfavorable" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt). **SAFE ON AIR:** "Waiting was deadlier than cutting. Men amputated in the first two days had the best odds."
27. Surgeons' manuals told them to amputate within the first two days. — "Surgical manuals taught that an amputation should be performed within the first two days following injury." — [National Museum of Health and Medicine, "To Bind Up the Nation's Wounds"](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_02)
28. Why surgeons amputated: the Minié ball shattered bone and drove debris into the wound. — "splintered bones, damaged muscle, and drove dirt, clothing, and other debris into the wounds." — [Backus, "Amputations and the Civil War," battlefields.org](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/amputations-and-civil-war)
29. Why surgeons amputated, continued: with nerves and vessels destroyed, the limb could not be saved. — "When the nerves and vessels were damaged, amputation gave the best chance of survival." — [Slawson, NMCWM "The Story of the Pile of Limbs"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/surgeons-call/limbs/)
30. How fast an amputation was done (NPS). — "The average amputation could be finished in 10-15 minutes" — [NPS, "Military Medicine at Wilson's Creek"](https://www.nps.gov/articles/military-medicine-at-wilson-s-creek.htm). The NMCWM says "only a few minutes" ([Slawson](https://www.civilwarmed.org/surgeons-call/limbs/)). **SAFE ON AIR:** "An experienced surgeon could finish an amputation in roughly ten to fifteen minutes."
31. Amputation also served triage, because it was the fastest way to deal with mass casualties. — "It was the fastest way to treat the largest number of wounded in a very short window of time." — [NMCWM (Slawson)](https://www.civilwarmed.org/surgeons-call/limbs/)
32. Amputations made up a large share of all surgery. — "three out of four surgeries (or close to 60,000 operations) were amputations." — [battlefields.org](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/amputations-and-civil-war) **[DISAGREE]** "60,000" covers both armies. The Union-only recorded figure is 29,980 (MSH), and Confederate amputations are put at about 25,000 ([Schroeder-Lein, Essential Civil War Curriculum](https://essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/the-wounded.html)) or about 28,000 ([Henretta/Keen](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/henretta8e/henretta8e_ch14_10.html)). **SAFE ON AIR:** "Union surgeons recorded nearly 30,000 amputations. Counting both sides, the total was perhaps 50,000 to 60,000."
33. Excision (cutting out only the shattered bone) was usually deadlier than amputation. — "the excisions are uniformly followed by larger percentages of fatality than the amputations" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt). The MSH names the forearm and leg as exceptions.

## 3. Infection without germ theory

34. Surgeons had no idea germs existed. W. W. Keen, Union surgeon, said so in 1918. — "In the Civil War surgeons knew absolutely nothing of 'germs.'" — [NMCWM, "Post-War Speech by W.W. Keen" (reprint of *St. Louis Post-Dispatch*, Nov. 3, 1918)](https://www.civilwarmed.org/keen-surgery/)
35. Surgeons operated in blood-stained coats. — "They operated in old blood-stained coats, the veterans of a hundred fights." — Keen, [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/keen-surgery/)
36. Instruments were not disinfected and sponges were reused between patients. — "used marine sponges which had been used in prior cases and had been only washed in tap water." — Keen, [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/keen-surgery/)
37. Dropped instruments went straight back into use. — "If a sponge or an instrument fell on the floor it was washed and squeezed in a basin of tap water" — Keen, [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/keen-surgery/)
38. "Laudable pus": surgeons' own case records treat creamy pus as a good sign. — "the discharge now consisted of laudable pus." — case history in [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt). A modern summary: "Clean white pus was expected in a normal healing wound." — [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/surgery-civil-war)
39. Four deadly wound infections were recognized: hospital gangrene, erysipelas, pyemia and tetanus. — "four deadly secondary infections were recognized" — [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/surgery-civil-war)
40. **Pyemia (blood poisoning) killed 97.4% of the men who got it.** — "The total number of cases of pyemia after shot wounds was … (2,818), of which seventy-one (71) recovered … a mortality rate of 97.4 per cent." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt) **[DISAGREE]** The Essential Civil War Curriculum gives about 90%. Use the MSH figure. **SAFE ON AIR:** "Of 2,818 recorded cases of pyemia, only 71 men survived."
41. Nothing worked against established pyemia. — "In the well established cases of pyaemia all remedies were found ineffective." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
42. **Tetanus: 505 cases, 451 deaths (89.3%).** It was rare, about 2 per 1,000 wounds. — "five hundred and five (0.20 per cent., or a little over two in a thousand) were followed by tetanus." and "Of the five hundred and five cases, four hundred and fifty-one, or 89.3 per cent., ended" fatally — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
43. **Erysipelas: 1,097 cases, 450 deaths (41.0%).** — "Four hundred and fifty of the one thousand and ninety-seven cases of erysipelas proved fatal, a mortality rate of 41.0 per cent." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
44. **Gangrene of all kinds after shot wounds: 2,642 cases, 45.6% mortality where the outcome is known.** — "one thousand one hundred and forty-two (1,142) in death, a mortality rate of 45.6 per cent." — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt). Caveat: the MSH says surgeons used the gangrene terms "indiscriminately", so this figure lumps hospital gangrene together with other gangrene. The UT Health San Antonio library page calls 45.6% the mortality of "hospital gangrene"; that is a slight over-reading.
45. Hospital gangrene was worst in large city hospitals later in the war. — "was most common in larger urban hospitals later in the war." — [Schroeder-Lein, ECWC "The Wounded"](https://essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/the-wounded.html)
46. **Bromine:** Union Surgeon Middleton Goldsmith treated hospital gangrene with bromine. Only 8 of 304 bromine-treated patients died (2.6%). — "only 8 of Goldsmith's 304 patients receiving bromine-based therapy for gangrene died, a mortality rate of only 2.6 per cent." — [UT Health San Antonio Library, "Middleton Goldsmith and the Use of Bromine"](https://library.uthscsa.edu/2014/10/middleton-goldsmith-and-the-use-of-bromine-to-treat-gangrene/). The MSH reprints Goldsmith's table of 334 cases from Louisville, Nashville, Murfreesboro and New Albany ([MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)). **SAFE ON AIR:** "In Goldsmith's own series, bromine cut deaths to a few percent." Caveat: these are his self-reported, uncontrolled results.
47. Goldsmith's method as described in the MSH: bromine applied directly to the sore, sometimes injected around it. — "It consists in the direct local application of bromine, either pure or in solution, to the surface of the sloughing sore." — Surgeon J. H. Brinton, in [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
48. A patient's case note on bromine: it worked, and it stung. — "Bromine has acted like a charm, and still possesses a biting character when applied" — case report in [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
49. Lister's antiseptic method was published in 1867, two years after the war, and used carbolic acid. — "The material which I have employed is carbolic or phenic acid" — [Lister, "Antiseptic Principle of the Practice of Surgery," 1867 (Fordham Modern History Sourcebook)](https://sourcebooks.web.fordham.edu/mod/1867lister.asp)

## 4. Abdomen, chest and head wounds (numbers only)

50. **Penetrating abdominal wounds: 87.2% mortality.** Of 3,475 cases with a known outcome, 3,031 died. — "of thirty-four hundred and seventy-five determined penetrating wounds of the abdomen, 3,031 deaths, or 87.2 per cent." — [MSH-Surg2](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX5.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX5_djvu.txt)
51. Flesh wounds of the abdomen that did not penetrate had 7.8% mortality. — "Of thirty-three hundred and seventy-three determined flesh wounds of the abdomen, 266 deaths, or 7.8 per cent." — [MSH-Surg2](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX5.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX5_djvu.txt)
52. **Penetrating chest wounds: 62.4% mortality.** Of 7,654 Union cases with a known outcome, 4,782 died. — "there remain seven thousand six hundred and fifty-four determined cases of Union soldiers, with four thousand seven hundred and eighty-two deaths, or 62.4 per cent." — [MSH-Surg1](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX4.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX4_djvu.txt)
53. The Confederate chest-wound rate in the same MSH data was almost identical. — "gunshot wounds of the chest in Confederate soldiers is therefore 63.8 per cent." — [MSH-Surg1](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX4.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX4_djvu.txt)
54. **Gunshot injuries of the skull:** 4,350 were reported, not counting men killed outright. Of the 4,243 with known outcome, 59.2% died. — "there remain four thousand two hundred and forty-three cases, with a general mortality of 59.2 per cent" — [MSH-Surg1](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX4.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX4_djvu.txt). Note: this group includes contusions and fractures of the outer table only.
55. Major artery surgery was grim. Ligating the femoral artery had over 75% mortality. — "Of 374 ligations of the femoral artery, 93 recovered and 281 died, a mortality of over 75 per cent." — W. W. Keen (1905), quoted in [Henretta/Macmillan](https://digfir-published.macmillanusa.com/henretta8e/henretta8e_ch14_10.html)

**SAFE ON AIR for the torso:** "A bullet through the belly killed nearly nine in ten. Through the chest, roughly six in ten. Through the skull, about six in ten of those who reached a hospital."

## 5. General hospitals, trains and ships

56. **At its peak the Union had 204 general hospitals with 136,894 beds.** — "a maximum of 204 general hospitals, with a capacity of 136,894 beds, was reached." — [Surgeon General's report, *Official Records* Ser. III, Vol. V, p. 523 (civilwar.com transcription)](https://civilwar.com/official-record/990-union-letters-orders-reports/273595-523-series-iii-volume-v-serial-126-union-letters-orders-reports.html). The same figure appears in [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/hospitals-civil-war).
57. **General hospitals treated 1,057,423 cases, with 8% mortality.** — "1,057,423 cases have been treated in general hospitals alone from 1861 to July 1, 1865" and "the rate of mortality was 8 per cent." — [*Official Records* Ser. III, Vol. V, p. 523](https://civilwar.com/official-record/990-union-letters-orders-reports/273595-523-series-iii-volume-v-serial-126-union-letters-orders-reports.html). The American Battlefield Trust also says 8% ([battlefields.org, "Civil War Medicine"](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-medicine)). **SAFE ON AIR:** "More than a million cases passed through Union general hospitals. About eight in a hundred died, which was remarkably low for the time." Caveat: the transcription page does not show the signer; it is attributed to the Surgeon General's office.
58. Pavilion design: long, narrow wards with windows in facing pairs for cross-ventilation, following Florence Nightingale's Crimean War lessons. — "incorporated multiple windows located in opposing pairs for cross-ventilation." — [NMCWM, "Pavilion Hospitals"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/pavilion-hospitals/)
59. Satterlee (West Philadelphia) opened in June 1862 with more than 3,500 beds and was a pet project of Surgeon General Hammond. — "opened in June of 1862 with 36 pavilions containing more than 3,500 beds." — [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/pavilion-hospitals/)
60. Mower (Chestnut Hill, Philadelphia) had about 3,600 beds and treated over 200,000 patients. — "it had some 3,600 beds and treated over 200,000 patients." — [Pennsylvania historical marker, hmdb.org](https://www.hmdb.org/m.asp?m=6334) **[ESTIMATE]** The 200,000 figure is the marker's. Treat it with caution, since it is high against the 1,057,423 total for all general hospitals.
61. Washington became a city of hospitals. Whitman wrote of "thirty or forty" hospital clusters holding up to 70,000 men. — "Within sight of the capitol, as I write, are some thirty or forty such collections, at times holding from fifty to seventy thousand men." — [Whitman, *Specimen Days* (1882–83), archive.org](https://archive.org/download/specimendayscoll00whit_0/specimendayscoll00whit_0_djvu.txt) **[ESTIMATE]** Whitman's own estimate.
62. Hospital trains: more than 25,000 men were evacuated north by rail. — "Trains were used to evacuate more than 25,000 men to hospitals in the North." — [NMHM, "To Bind Up the Nation's Wounds," p. 4](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_04)
63. The hospital steamer *D. A. January* had nearly 450 beds and carried more than 23,000 wounded over four years. — "During its four years of service, the DA January transported and cared for more than 23,000 wounded men." — [NMHM](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_04)
64. **USS *Red Rover* was the Navy's first hospital ship.** — "Red Rover, the Navy's first hospital ship, was a side-wheel steamer" — [Naval History and Heritage Command, DANFS](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/r/red-rover.html). She took her first patient on June 11, 1862, and was commissioned on December 26, 1862.
65. *Red Rover* carried an operating room, laundry, elevator and bathrooms, and admitted over 2,400 patients. — "Having admitted over 2,400 patients during her career" — [NHHC DANFS](https://www.history.navy.mil/research/histories/ship-histories/danfs/r/red-rover.html) **[DISAGREE]** The NMCWM says "more than 2,000" ([Wynn](https://www.civilwarmed.org/redrover/)). The two also disagree on build year and place: DANFS says 1859, Cape Girardeau; NMCWM says 1857, Louisville. **SAFE ON AIR:** "more than two thousand patients."
66. Catholic Sisters of the Holy Cross served aboard *Red Rover*. NMCWM calls them the first women in Navy service. — "These became the first women in the service of the United States Navy." — [NMCWM, "Mercy on the Mississippi"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/redrover/)

## 6. Nurses

67. Dorothea Dix was appointed Superintendent of Army Nurses in June 1861. — "was appointed by the United States government to serve as the Superintendent of Army Nurses in June of 1861." — [battlefields.org, Dix biography](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/biographies/dorothea-lynde-dix). Dix's rules said nurses should be "plain-looking" and middle-aged. — "She required nurses to be between the ages of 35 and 50, and to be "plain-looking."" — [battlefields.org](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/biographies/dorothea-lynde-dix) **[DISAGREE]** The Smithsonian Archives says "over thirty" ("requiring that all of her nurses be over thirty, plain looking, and wear dull uniforms" — [SIA](https://siarchives.si.edu/blog/dorothea-dix-mental-health-reformer-and-civil-war-nurse)). 71. Pay: women nurses earned 40 cents a day, or $12 a month by law. — "pay them $12 a month and provide them with food rations." — [U.S. Army Heritage & Education Center](https://www.armyheritage.org/?p=10041). 73. **Clara Barton at Antietam (her own words):** a bullet passed through her sleeve and killed the man she was helping. — "A ball has passed between my body and the right arm which supported him, cutting through his chest" — [NPS Antietam, "Clara Barton at Antietam"](https://www.nps.gov/anti/learn/historyculture/clarabarton.htm). ## 7. Disease

85. **The Union's official count of deaths was 304,369 by 1870.** Of these, 44,238 were killed in battle, 49,205 died of wounds and injuries, and 186,216 died of disease. — "The total number of deaths of this class entered on the books being 186,216" (disease) — [MSH-Med1, Introduction (Woodward, 1870)](https://archive.org/download/MSHWRMedical1/MSHWRMedical1_djvu.txt). Killed in battle: "This report gives the number of Killed in Battle as 44,238". Total: "of the whole number of 304,369 deaths known to have occurred during the War". All from the same page.
86. **The ratio check for our Short's "2:1":** disease deaths (186,216) against battle deaths (44,238 + 49,205 = 93,443) is almost exactly 2 to 1. **The Short is consistent with the MSH.** Disease accounts for 61% of all 304,369 recorded deaths, or about 66% if the 24,184 deaths from unknown causes are left out. Modern summaries say "two out of every three" ([NPS, "The Reason Medical Practices Changed"](https://www.nps.gov/articles/the-reason-medical-practices-changed.htm): "Two out of every three deaths in the Civil War were caused by disease rather than injury."). **SAFE ON AIR:** "For every Union soldier killed by the enemy, roughly two died of disease," or "about two of every three deaths came from disease." Caveat: modern scholars put total war deaths higher than the 1870 count, so cite these as "the army's own records".
87. Diarrhea and dysentery were the top killers: 37,794 white and 6,764 Black Union soldiers died of them, 44,558 in total. — "37,794 deaths of white and 6,764 of Colored troops were due to the several forms of Diarrhoea and Dysentery" — [MSH-Med1](https://archive.org/download/MSHWRMedical1/MSHWRMedical1_djvu.txt)
88. Recorded diarrhea and dysentery cases numbered about 1.7 million. — Montana State University's table, citing the MSH, gives "1,739,135" cases and 44,558 deaths ([MSU "Diseases of Entomological Importance"](https://montana.edu/historybug/civil_war_disease_table.html)). **[DISAGREE]** Burns (PBS) gives "The total recorded Union cases was 1,528,098" and about 57,000 deaths ([PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/disease)). The likely reason is that one figure counts white troops only and the other all troops, and Burns adds deaths from chronic forms. **SAFE ON AIR:** "well over a million cases of diarrhea and dysentery, and more than 44,000 deaths."
89. Camp fevers (typhoid, typhus, remittent, typho-malarial and others) killed 35,965 white and 4,691 Black Union soldiers. — "To these the tables attribute 35,965 deaths of White, and 4,691 of Colored troops." — [MSH-Med1](https://archive.org/download/MSHWRMedical1/MSHWRMedical1_djvu.txt)
90. Typhoid alone: 148,631 Union cases and 34,833 deaths. — "There were 148,631 cases in Union troops with 34,833 deaths." — [Burns, PBS "Disease"](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/disease). The MSU table gives the same figures.
91. Malaria: about 1.3 million cases and about 10,000 deaths. — "The second most common disease was malaria with over 1.3 million cases and more then 10,000 deaths." (sic) — [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/disease). MSU gives 1,315,955 cases and 10,063 deaths.
92. Measles, which hit new rural recruits hardest: 76,318 cases and 5,177 deaths. — "Measles infected 76,318, killing 5,177." — [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/disease) (secondary source; the primary MSH table was not checked).
93. Smallpox killed 7,058 of 18,952 cases, even though a vaccine existed. — "Small pox, for which a vaccine was available, killed 7,058 of the 18,952 infected." — [Burns, PBS](https://www.pbs.org/mercy-street/uncover-history/behind-lens/disease)
94. Pneumonia killed 14,738 white and 5,233 Black Union soldiers. — "Pneumonia, of which 14,738 deaths of White, and 5,233 of Colored troops arc reported." (OCR "arc" = "are") — [MSH-Med1](https://archive.org/download/MSHWRMedical1/MSHWRMedical1_djvu.txt)

## 8. Aftermath: limbs, pensions, museum, MSH

95. Just under 30,000 Union soldiers lost a limb, and over 21,000 survived the operation. — "just under 30,000 Union soldiers lost a limb during the war" — [NMCWM, "After the Amputation"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/prosthetics/)
96. Washington paid for artificial limbs, or a cash commutation of up to $75, for documented Union amputees. — "Washington would provide an artificial limb or a financial commutation of up to seventy-five dollars." — [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/prosthetics/)
97. Confederate veterans were excluded from the federal program, so Southern states ran their own. — "Some former Confederate states like North Carolina and Virginia funded their own artificial limb programs" — [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/prosthetics/)
98. North Carolina's program ran until 1871 and spent $81,310.12. — "The state program ran until 1871, spending $81,310.12…." — [UNC Libraries, North Carolina Collection blog](https://blogs.lib.unc.edu/ncm/2012/12/21/)
99. **Mississippi, 1866: [UNVERIFIED / DISAGREE].** Collectors Weekly (via Bunk History) says the state "spent more than half its yearly budget providing veterans with artificial limbs" ([Bunk History](https://www.bunkhistory.org/resources/2921)). A blog citing historian David Oshinsky says "one-fifth of the state budget" ([Lawyers, Guns & Money](https://lawyersgunsmoneyblog.com/2014/11/last-time-mississippi-will-subsidize-health-care)). The Mississippi archives (MDAH) only list "Correspondence & circulars about artificial limbs: 1866" ([MDAH Series 402](https://finding.mdah.ms.gov/government-records/402)). **DO NOT USE A PERCENTAGE.** **SAFE ON AIR:** "In 1866 Mississippi spent a large part of its budget on artificial limbs for its veterans." Even this is safer cut, or rewritten as "Southern states, shut out of federal help, paid for limbs themselves" (fact 97).
100. **The empty sleeve:** many men refused a hook. — "many veterans with a missing arm preferred an empty sleeve to a metal hook." — [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/prosthetics/)
101. The left-handed penmanship contests (1865–67), run by William Oland Bourne for Union men who had lost their right arms, drew 270 entrants to the first contest and 113 to the second. — "The right arm's gone," wrote veteran Alfred Whitehouse. — [NMCWM, "The Left-Armed Corps"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/left/). [Library of Congress, Bourne Papers](https://www.loc.gov/collections/wm-oland-bourne-papers/about-this-collection/) gives "$1,000 in prizes" for the first contest.
102. Pensions: the law of July 14, 1862 gave a totally disabled private $8 a month, and loss of a limb counted as total disability. — "provided an $8 a month pension to private soldiers" and "Loss of limb or its extremity was always rated as a total disability" — [National Archives, *Prologue*, Spring 2010 (Prechtel-Kluskens)](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2010/spring/civilwarpension.html)
103. Pension costs grew from $1 million a year in 1860 to $36 million in 1885, with nearly 325,000 people on the rolls 20 years after the war. — "Annual pension payment costs of $1 million annually in 1860 ballooned to $36 million in 1885." — [*Prologue*](https://www.archives.gov/publications/prologue/2010/spring/civilwarpension.html)
104. **The Army Medical Museum** was founded in 1862 by Surgeon General William Hammond. It is now the National Museum of Health and Medicine (renamed 1989). — "Surgeon General William Hammond established the Army Medical Museum" — [NMHM History](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/history)
105. Surgeons were ordered to send specimens. A June 24, 1864 circular reads: — "Medical Officers in charge of Hospitals are directed to diligently collect and preserve for the Army Medical Museum, all pathological surgical specimens" — [MSH-Med1](https://archive.org/download/MSHWRMedical1/MSHWRMedical1_djvu.txt)
106. **The MSH:** six volumes, about 3,000 pages, weighing about 55 pounds. Publication began in 1870 and the last volume appeared in 1888. — "almost 3,000 pages and weighed 55 pounds" — [McGill Osler Library, "The Medical and Surgical History of the War of the Rebellion"](https://www.mcgill.ca/medicalmuseum/node/412) **[DISAGREE]** The NMHM history page says "between 1870 and 1883", and McGill says the last volume came in 1883. Archive.org records show the final *surgical* volume dated 1883 ([MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)) and the third *medical* volume (Charles Smart) dated 1888. **SAFE ON AIR:** "published in six volumes between 1870 and 1888."
107. Why the MSH mattered: it held thousands of case reports and became a reference used worldwide. — "a spectacular source of medical information, with thousands of case reports." — [Dorwart, ECWC "Civil War Medicine"](https://www.essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/civil-war-medicine.html). William Osler is quoted as calling Woodward's diarrhea volume "a perfect mine of information" ([McGill](https://www.mcgill.ca/medicalmuseum/node/412)).
108. Hammond's purpose for the museum (as quoted by NMCWM). — "preserves, for future application, the dearly bought experience of four years of war" — [NMCWM, "The Civil War and the Army Medical Museum"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/army-medical-museum/)
109. General Dan Sickles' amputated leg: his amputation healed fast and he returned to service by September 1863. He was photographed at the Army Medical Museum in 1865. — "Sickles was photographed in 1865 at the Army Medical Museum." — [NMHM](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_02)

## 9. What the war changed (closing chapter)

110. The war left a medical legacy of seven advances (ECWC list): triage, an efficient ambulance system, mobile hospitals, the Army Medical Museum, the MSH, *Index Medicus*, and the Surgeon General's Library (now the National Library of Medicine). — "At least seven advances comprise the medical legacy of the Civil War." — [Dorwart, ECWC](https://www.essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/civil-war-medicine.html)
111. Anesthesia became standard. Its use in more than 80,000 cases made it routine in American surgery (fact 1). Good framing line from the MSH: — "The inestimable value of the use of anaesthetics in military surgery will hardly be denied at this date" — [MSH-Surg3](https://archive.org/download/14121350RX6.nlm.nih.gov/14121350RX6_djvu.txt)
112. Hospital design: pavilion hospitals brought death rates down to a level then unheard of. — "these hospitals had a previously unheard of 8% mortality rate for their patients." — [battlefields.org, "Civil War Medicine" (Dixon)](https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/civil-war-medicine)
113. Wards divided by disease were a key innovation. — "One key innovation during this period was the division of hospitals into wards based on disease." — [Smithsonian Magazine, "Six Ways the Civil War Changed American Medicine" (Thompson)](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/six-ways-civil-war-changed-american-medicine-180955626/)
114. A change in mindset. — "The real lasting impact was the change in mindset of both doctors and the people who they were treating" — "Koyle" (as named on the page; full name not captured), quoted in [Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/six-ways-civil-war-changed-american-medicine-180955626/)
115. Nursing became a profession. Mary Livermore, a wartime nursing leader, later praised its professionalization. — "Livermore concluded with praise the progress that had been made in the professionalization of nursing since the Civil War." — [Egenes, ECWC "Civil War Nursing"](https://essentialcivilwarcurriculum.com/civil-war-nursing.html) (No source opened for the "first U.S. nursing schools in 1873" claim, so do not use it.)
116. Record keeping: the Surgeon General's Office built enormous case registers that later fed the MSH. — "over sixteen thousand folio volumes of manuscript record books" — [NMCWM, "The Civil War and the Army Medical Museum"](https://www.civilwarmed.org/army-medical-museum/)
117. Prosthetics: the war created a new industry. Between 1845 and 1861 only 34 prosthetic-limb patents were issued. — "Between 1845 and 1861, 34 patents were issued for prosthetic limbs" — [NMCWM](https://www.civilwarmed.org/prosthetics/). The A. J. Watson adjustable steel leg was patented in 1867 ([NMHM](https://medicalmuseum.health.mil/index.cfm/visit/exhibits/past/nationswounds/page_02)).
