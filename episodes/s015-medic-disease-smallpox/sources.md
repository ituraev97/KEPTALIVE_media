## s1 — Waverly Woodson, D-Day medic (30 hours)
- Health.mil (DHA, 2024-06-04), "D-Day Medic Waverly Woodson Saved Lives at Normandy Beach; Awarded Distinguished Service Cross": https://health.mil/News/Dvids-Articles/2024/06/04/news472898
  - "Cpl. Waverly B. Woodson Jr., 21," "assigned to the 320th Barrage Balloon Battalion, First Army"
  - "Woodson's landing craft tank took heavy fire, hit a naval mine, lost power, and drifted to shore."
  - "Woodson was gravely wounded by mortar shrapnel tearing through his groin and back."
  - "some accounts put the number at about 200 service members"; "Woodson worked continuously for 30 hours before collapsing from his injuries, pain, and blood loss"
  - Earlier: Bronze Star and Combat Medic Badge presented to his widow and son on Oct. 11, 2023. Woodson died in 2005.
- Army.mil article 277628, "Waverly Woodson: a legacy of distinguished service recognized at Rock Island Arsenal": https://www.army.mil/article/277628/waverly_woodson_a_legacy_of_distinguished_service_recognized_at_rock_island_arsenal
  - Omaha Beach, D-Day June 6, 1944; posthumously awarded the DSC on June 3, 2024 (Maj. Gen. William Ryan placed the medal on the sand at Omaha Beach).
  - Historical commission quote: "For 30 continuous hours while under enemy fire, Woodson cared for more than 200 casualties." Relieved at 4:00 p.m. on June 7.
  - Gave artificial respiration to three men who had gone underwater during a landing attempt.
- DVIDS 1010687 "DDay Medic Waverly Woodson" (Sean Metzger, Defense Health Agency; filmed/published 2026-06-12/17): archival portraits and D-Day photographs; its own on-screen text also states June 3, 2024 DSC, "second-highest U.S. military honor", "close to 200 wounded … for almost 30 hours". ## s2 — Civil War: disease vs. battle
- National Park Service, "Death and Dying": https://www.nps.gov/stories/death-and-dying.htm
  - "The number of soldiers who died between 1861 and 1865, generally estimated at 620,000"
  - "Twice as many Civil War soldiers died from disease as from battle wounds"
- American Battlefield Trust, "Fight for Life or Death": https://battlefields.org/learn/articles/fight-life-or-death
  - "Over the course of the Civil War, two-thirds of soldiers' deaths were caused by disease and infection, rather than injuries."
  - "illnesses such as dysentery, typhoid, measles and smallpox"; "a crowded, unsanitary environment"
  - John B. Gordon (Confederate officer): "it was amazing to see the large number of country boys who had never had the measles."
  - "limited knowledge of germs"

## s3 — Smallpox inoculation of the Continental Army, 1777
- National Park Service, "Smallpox Inoculation in the Revolutionary War": https://www.nps.gov/articles/000/smallpox-inoculation-revolutionary-war.htm
  - Washington to Dr. William Shippen Jr., Feb. 6, 1777: "I have determined that the Troops shall be inoculated."
  - Inoculation = "the process of contracting smallpox on purpose to induce immunity"; material from a pustule inserted through a small incision, usually causing mild, non-life-threatening symptoms.
  - Inoculations kept secret so the British would not learn of a weakened army; by late 1777 the disease was substantially reduced.
- American Battlefield Trust, "Washington Inoculates an Army": https://www.battlefields.org/learn/articles/washington-inoculates-army
  - Feb. 6, 1777: Washington ordered the Continental Army inoculated; "we should have more to dread from it, than from the Sword of the Enemy."
  - "Smallpox had ravaged the American Army that invaded Canada in the winter of 1775-1776."; carried out covertly so the British were unaware.
- Portrait: Gilbert Stuart, George Washington (Athenaeum portrait), 1796, National Portrait Gallery; Internet Archive item gilbert-stuart-george-washington-the-athenaeum-portrait-google-art-project_202412 (Public Domain Mark). Painted 19 years after the events — labelled "PORTRAIT 1796".
