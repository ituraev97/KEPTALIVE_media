## s1 — WWI blood depot (Oswald H. Robertson, U.S. Army)
- KUMC WWI archives, "Blood transfusion": https://www.kumc.edu/school-of-medicine/academics/departments/history-and-philosophy-of-medicine/archives/wwi/essays/medicine/blood-transfusion.html
  - "Among them were Roger Lee and Oswald Hope Robertson, with Base Hospital No. 5 from Boston"
  - "There he drew up plans for what many consider the world's first blood bank."
  - "He selected only group O blood donors, compatible with all other blood types, thus requiring no further testing."
  - "Initially, Robertson used citrated blood drawn into one liter glass bottles" / "Robertson soon incorporated Rous and Turner's dextrose into his bottles."
  - "converting ammunition boxes into shipping containers, with sawdust and ice packed around the bottles."
- The National Archives (UK) blog, "How three men saved countless lives": https://blog.nationalarchives.gov.uk/three-men-saved-countless-lives-blood-transfusion-first-world-war
  - "joined the Western Front as a volunteer with the United States Army" · "storing blood mixed with a citrate and dextrose solution in glass bottles kept on ice." · "The blood could be kept for up to 26 days" · "He built the world's first blood bank"
- Proto (Mass General): https://protomag-dev.partners.org/technology/blood-depot/
  - "Robertson was called into service as a member of the Army Medical Corps." · "with type O blood, which could safely be given to most soldiers regardless of their blood type." · "treated with sodium citrate, recently shown to be an effective anticoagulant." · "The result was the first "blood depot"—the precursor of the modern blood bank—in 1917." · "The system was put to the test in the Battle of Cambrai on the Western Front."

## s2 — Vietnam → TCCC → tourniquets
- Butler FK, Blackbourne LH. "Battlefield trauma care then and now: a decade of Tactical Combat Casualty Care." J Trauma Acute Care Surg 2012;73(6 Suppl 5):S395–S402: https://www.east.org/content/documents/MilitaryResources/b/TCCC%20Butler%20A%20Decade%20of%20TCCC%20J%20Trauma%202012.pdf
  - p. S395: "193 of a cohort of 2,600 casualties that were killed in action in Vietnam died of isolated extremity hemorrhage." (193/2,600 = 7.4%; the same paper's text says 7.9%, the 2015 paper says 7.4% → we use 7.4% with "193 of 2,600 studied")
  - p. S396: "'Tactical Combat Casualty Care in Special Operations,' which was published as a supplement to the journal Military Medicine in August 1996." Ref 4: Butler FK, Hagmann J, Butler EG.
  - Table 1 (2001 practice): "Not to use tourniquets to control extremity hemorrhage"
  - Table 2: "March 2005 US Army Surgeon General directs that CAT tourniquets be issued to all Soldiers deploying in support of combat operations in OEF/OIF."
  - Table 2: "2009 Based on COL Kragh's research, an estimate that 1,000–2,000 lives have been saved to date in US casualties by the use of battlefield tourniquets"
- Butler FK. J Spec Oper Med 2015;15(4):149–152: https://jsomonline.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/20154149Butler.pdf
  - p. 149, quoting the 1996 paper: "Although ATLS [Advanced Trauma Life Support] discourages the use of tourniquets, they are appropriate in this instance" · "This incidence of death from extremity hemorrhage was essentially unchanged from the 7.4 percent noted in Vietnam" · "the number of lives saved from this intervention has been estimated to be between 1,000 and 2,000."
- Wilderness Environ Med 2017 abstract (PubMed 28284483): https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28284483/ — "The product of that 3-year research project was TCCC, the first-ever set of battlefield trauma care guidelines designed to combine good medicine with good small-unit tactics."

## s3 — Korea, Bell H-13 litter pods
- army.mil, "Army helicopters in Korea, 1950 to '53" (Dr. Kaylene Hughes, 2016-10-28): https://www.army.mil/article/177302/army_helicopters_in_korea_1950_to_53
  - "The Army helicopters initially deployed to Korea were the Bell H-13 Sioux and the Hiller H-23 Raven" · "within the two exterior pods with which the Sioux and Raven helicopters were equipped."
  - "on the third day of that month [January 1951], 1st Lt. Willis G. Shawn and 1st Lt. Joseph L. Bowler flew the first Army aerial medical evacuation (MEDEVAC) missions."
  - "the aviators used the H-13 to transport 18,000 of the war's total 23,000 casualties to forward deployed mobile Army surgical hospitals."
