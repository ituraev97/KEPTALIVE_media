## Facts by Short (printed page)
**s1 flu (p. 125–127, Diagram 56)**
- "Up to September 14, 1918, only 9,840 deaths from disease had occurred in the Army, and the death rate for the period of the war up to that time was only 5 per year for each 1,000 men." (p. 125)
- "During the eight weeks from September 14 to the 8th of November 316,089 cases of influenza and 53,449 of pneumonia were reported among troops in this country." (p. 125–126)
- Diagram 56 "weekly death rate for each 1,000 troops in this country during the year 1918… high point the second week in October, when 4 out of each 1,000 troops under arms in this country died." (p. 126–127)
- "More than 40,000 died of the disease [pneumonia]. Of these, probably 25,000 resulted from the influenza-pneumonia pandemic" (p. 125; whole war to May 1, 1919). Summary item 11, p. 130: "Pneumonia killed more soldiers than were killed in battle." (compares with killed in battle only)
- Our wording keeps "troops at home / in the United States" and "probably"; 5 is a per-YEAR rate, 4 is a per-WEEK rate (the chart's own units, different periods).
**s2 wounded (summary, p. 130, items 1, 6, 7; p. 125)**
- Item 6 "For every man killed in battle six were wounded." Item 7 "Five out of every six men sent to hospitals on account of wounds were cured and returned to duty." Item 1 "Of every 100 American soldiers and sailors, who served in the war with Germany, two were killed or died of disease during the period of hostilities."
- p. 125: "2,089 commissioned medical officers, including the Reserves [at the beginning of the war]. During the war 31,251 physicians from civil life were commissioned in the Medical Corps."
**s3 ladder (p. 124, Diagram 54)**
- "The death rate from disease in the Mexican War was 110 per year in each 1,000 men; in the Civil War this was reduced to 65; and in the Spanish War to 26; while the rate in the expeditionary forces in this war was 19. The battle rate of 53 for the overseas forces is higher than in any previous war." Diagram 54 labels: Mexican War 1846–48, Civil War (North) 1861–65, Spanish War 1898, present war to Nov. 11, 1918.
- p. 125: causes of the low disease death rate: highly trained medical personnel, compulsory vaccination against typhoid fever, camp sanitation and control of drinking water, adequate hospital facilities. (Our line: "trained doctors, typhoid vaccination, clean camps, and hospitals" = this list, condensed.)
**s4 totals + typhoid (p. 123–125, Diagram 53)**
- "The total number of lives lost in both Army and Navy… is 125,500. Deaths in the Army, including marines attached to it, were 115,660." Diagram 53 (Total 115,660): disease 50 % = 57,460; battle 43 % = 50,280; other 7 % = 7,920 ("Other" includes accident). 
- "If the comparison between disease and battle losses is limited to the expeditionary forces, battle losses appear more than twice as large as deaths from disease." (p. 124; summary item 8 p. 130)
- Typhoid: "During the entire war up to May 1, 1919, a total of only 2,328 cases of typhoid fever have been reported and only 227 deaths from this cause. The result is due to the compulsory vaccination of every man who entered the Army and to excellent sanitary conditions." (p. 125)
